## I. THE PATIENT

### S10 · Act title
**Picture:** `GFX: title` I · THE PATIENT

### S11 · One paper
**Picture:** `GFX: document` Recreated title block of the paper: authors, journal, year, "University of Virginia." [A]
> Almost everything we know about this man comes from one paper, written by two neurologists at the University of Virginia, Jeffrey Burns and Russell Swerdlow. It was published in 2003. It's four pages long, and it's more careful than the retellings, so we're going to stay close to it.

### S12 · The year 2000
**Picture:** `GFX: timeline` A timeline draws left to right from 2000; the "behavior" track lights up. [A4, A6]
> In 2000, he began collecting pornography and visiting massage parlors for sex. He hid all of it, and the paper records why: he felt it was unacceptable. Remember that. It matters later.

### S13 · The choice
**Picture:** `GFX: timeline` Markers: "Removed from home," "Found guilty," "Treatment or jail," "Expelled." [A7, A8]
> When his stepdaughter told her mother, he was removed from the home and found guilty. A judge gave him a choice: an inpatient treatment program, or jail. He chose treatment, and then got himself expelled, because he couldn't stop propositioning the staff and the other patients.

### S14 · The exam
**Picture:** `3D: corridor` Corridor again, slower; a door at the end. [A10]
> Which brings us back to that hospital, the night before sentencing. Over the next day, the doctors noticed things that didn't fit a man who was simply depraved. His balance was off. He propositioned the women examining him. He had wet himself and didn't seem to care. Asked to copy a clock face, he couldn't do it.

### S15 · The tumor
**Picture:** `GFX: scan` Illustrative slice with the mass outlined and labelled "right orbitofrontal cortex." [A11]
> Those are signs of something physical. The scan found a tumor, a hemangiopericytoma, growing up from the base of his skull and pushing into the right orbitofrontal cortex. That's a region just above the eyes that helps weigh what we want against what it will cost us. It's part of the brain's system for saying: not now, not this.

### S16 · First surgery
**Picture:** `3D: brain` Tumor fades out. Timeline marker "Dec 2000: removed." [A12]
> It was removed in December 2000. Within days, his walking and bladder control improved. He completed a twelve-step program. Months later, he was judged no threat to his stepdaughter, and he went home.

### S17 · Recurrence
**Picture:** `GFX: timeline` "Oct 2001: headache, collecting again" → "Feb 2002: removed again." [A13, A14]
> In October 2001, he developed a persistent headache, and he began secretly collecting pornography again. A new scan showed the tumor had come back. It was removed again in February 2002, and, according to his doctors' accounts to the press, the urges went away a second time.

### S18 · On, off, on
**Picture:** `GFX: abab` Two tracks, TUMOR and BEHAVIOR, switching on, off, on, off in step. RECORD stamp. [A12–A14, A18]
> Look at the pattern. Tumor present, behavior present. Tumor gone, behavior gone. Tumor back, behavior back. On a single human being, that's about as close to an experiment as medicine gets. Swerdlow's own summary was blunt: what this man needed was an operation, not incarceration.

### S19 · First weight
**Picture:** `3D: scale` One card drops onto the left pan; the scale tips left.
> That's the first piece of evidence on the scale, and it's heavy.

### S20 · The fine print
**Picture:** `GFX: card` INFERENCE card: "The tumor likely made an existing interest worse. It didn't create one from nothing." Sources: Burns & Swerdlow 2003; Joyal 2023. [A16, A19, A20]
> But the paper also says something the popular version usually leaves out. The authors wrote that the tumor likely made worse a preexisting interest in pornography. It didn't create his desires out of nothing. And a 2023 review of sixty-four similar cases found that they mostly reflect a general loss of inhibition, not a new attraction. The tumor didn't write a new script. It may have fired the editor.

### S21 · The victim
**Picture:** `GFX: words` "A child was harmed." Then black.
> And one fact doesn't change, whatever we decide about him. A child was harmed. Everything else in this question sits on top of that.

## II. THE TEST

### S22 · Act title
**Picture:** `GFX: title` II · THE TEST

### S23 · 1843
**Picture:** `GFX: document` Recreated 1843 law-report page, letterpress serif. [B1]
> So, is he guilty? In most American courts, the answer starts with a rule written in England in 1843. A man named Daniel M'Naghten shot Edward Drummond, the private secretary of the Prime Minister, Robert Peel. Drummond died days later. He's generally believed to have mistaken Drummond for Peel himself. He was acquitted as insane, Queen Victoria was furious, and the judges were called before the House of Lords to explain themselves. What they wrote still decides cases today.

### S24 · The rule
**Picture:** `GFX: document` The test, marker sweeping "did not know he was doing what was wrong." [B2]
> A defendant is excused if, because of a disease of the mind, he didn't know what he was doing, or didn't know that it was wrong. That's the test. It's about knowledge.

### S25 · He knew
**Picture:** `3D: scale` A card drops on the right pan; the scale swings back toward level. [A6, A15, A17]
> And our man knew. The paper says his moral knowledge was intact. He hid what he did because he knew it was unacceptable. Swerdlow put it plainly: he knew what he was doing was wrong, and he didn't stop. Under the M'Naghten rule, he's sane. He's guilty.

### S26 · The other door
**Picture:** `GFX: document` Model Penal Code §4.01; marker sweeps "or to conform his conduct to the requirements of law." [B3]
> But there's another kind of test. In 1962, the American Law Institute proposed one with two questions. Could the defendant appreciate that his conduct was wrong? Or could he conform his conduct to the law? That second question is the only door that fits this man. His problem was never knowing. It was stopping.

### S27 · 1982
**Picture:** `GFX: words` "JUNE 21, 1982" → "NOT GUILTY BY REASON OF INSANITY" → "1984: INSANITY DEFENSE REFORM ACT." No images of people. [B4]
> For a while, that door was open in most federal courts. Then John Hinckley shot President Reagan, and in 1982 a jury found him not guilty by reason of insanity. The backlash was immediate. Two years later, Congress passed the Insanity Defense Reform Act, and the question of control was cut out of federal law.

### S28 · The map
**Picture:** `MAP: us_tests` US map: states that still ask about control (brass), states that abolished the defense (red outline), others grey. Caption "As of 2020 · Justice Breyer's count, *Kahler v. Kansas*." [B5–B7]
> Today, the answer depends on where you're standing. As of 2020, by Justice Stephen Breyer's count, sixteen states and Washington, D.C. still ask whether a defendant could control himself. Federal courts don't. And four states, Kansas, Idaho, Montana and Utah, have abolished the traditional insanity defense entirely. In 2020, the Supreme Court said that's allowed.

### S29 · Virginia
**Picture:** `MAP: us_tests` Push in to Virginia, highlighted. [A3, B5]
> And here's a twist. News reports describe our patient as a Virginia man. Virginia is one of only three states that pair the M'Naghten rule with a test for irresistible impulse. If his case had ever gone to an insanity trial, that argument was at least available. Whether a jury would have accepted it, nobody can say.

### S30 · How rare
**Picture:** `GFX: stat` "Raised in fewer than 1 in 100 felony cases" → "Succeeds in about 1 in 4 of those." Source line: Callahan et al. 1991, eight states. [B8]
> It's also worth knowing how rare all of this is. In a landmark study of eight states, the insanity defense was raised in under one percent of felony cases, and it succeeded in about a quarter of those. The idea of criminals walking free on an insanity plea is mostly a movie.

### S31 · Not satisfying
**Picture:** `3D: scale` The scale swings, settles almost level.
> So the law's answer is: it depends on the state, and on whether you think his problem was knowing or stopping. That isn't satisfying. So let's test the idea on a harder case.

## III. THE TOWER

### S32 · Act title
**Picture:** `GFX: title` III · THE TOWER

### S33 · Austin, 1966
**Picture:** `3D: tower` A tower at dawn, empty sky, slow push. Tag RECONSTRUCTION. No people, no event. [C]
> On the evening of July 31st, 1966, a twenty-five-year-old engineering student at the University of Texas sat down at a typewriter in Austin.

### S34 · The note
**Picture:** `GFX: document` Typed note excerpt, typing on. Header "TYPED NOTE · JULY 31, 1966 · 6:45 PM." [C2]
> "I don't really understand myself these days," he wrote. "I am supposed to be an average, reasonable and intelligent young man. However, lately, I have been a victim of many unusual and irrational thoughts."

### S35 · The request
**Picture:** `GFX: document` Same page, marker on the autopsy sentence. [C3]
> And then he asked for something unusual. "After my death," he wrote, "I wish that an autopsy would be performed on me to see if there is any visible physical disorder."

### S36 · What he did
**Picture:** `GFX: words` On black, names fade up one at a time: Claire Wilson · Thomas Eckman · Billy Paul Speed · … then "17 killed · 31 wounded · by today's count." No weapons, no photographs. [C1, C15]
> Over the next hours, he killed his mother and his wife. Then he carried a footlocker of weapons to the top of the university's tower. By today's count, he killed seventeen people, including an unborn child, and wounded thirty-one. Among them were Claire Wilson, eighteen years old and eight months pregnant, who survived; her son, who didn't; and her boyfriend, Thomas Eckman, who was killed moments later.

### S37 · The finding
**Picture:** `GFX: document` Recreated autopsy page, marker on "a fairly well outlined tumor about 2x1.5x1 cm." [C6]
> The autopsy found what he'd asked about: a tumor, about two centimeters across, deep in the middle of his brain.

### S38 · The legend
**Picture:** `3D: brain` Small glowing mass deep in the center. Text over: "THE STORY YOU'VE HEARD." [C10]
> That finding became one of the most famous stories in neuroscience. You may know it: a tumor pressing on his amygdala, the brain's threat center, turning a quiet young man into a killer. It's been told in books, lectures and viral videos.

### S39 · The record
**Picture:** `GFX: document` Autopsy line "No correlation to psychosis or permanent pains," highlighted. Then the five listed limits appear as a list. [C6, C7]
> Here's what the original documents actually say. The pathologist who did the autopsy called it an astrocytoma, and wrote: "No correlation to psychosis." A panel convened by the Governor of Texas re-examined the tissue, called it a glioblastoma, a highly malignant cancer, and listed five reasons its examination was limited. The body had already been embalmed. Parts of the brain were damaged or missing.

### S40 · The panel
**Picture:** `GFX: document` Two report quotes, one after the other, marker on "conceivably" and "does not enable us to explain." [C8, C9, C10]
> Their conclusion was careful. The tumor, they wrote, "conceivably could have contributed" to his inability to control his emotions and actions. But they also wrote that existing knowledge of the brain "does not enable us to explain" what he did. Neither document mentions the amygdala. That part of the story came later.

### S41 · The other pan
**Picture:** `3D: scale` Card after card drops on the right pan as each fact lands. [C5, C11, C13]
> Now look at the other side of the scale. Four months earlier, he told a university psychiatrist he'd been thinking about going up on the tower with a deer rifle and shooting people. He'd been up the tower twice that year. On the last morning, he bought guns at three different stores and packed food, water and supplies. He wrote down, hours ahead, that he had decided to kill his wife. He'd admitted to the psychiatrist that he had hit her before. And he described a father who beat his mother for years.

### S42 · Side by side
**Picture:** `GFX: compare` Two columns, VIRGINIA / AUSTIN: neurological signs (yes / none reported), behavior tracks tumor (twice / no data), planning (no / months). INFERENCE stamp. [A10–A14, C7, C11]
> Put the two cases side by side. In Virginia, the tumor came with obvious neurological signs, the behavior tracked the tumor, and it went away twice when the tumor did. In Austin, the people who knew him described no neurological symptoms, there's no before and after, and the planning looks like a mind that was working, not one that had lost control.

### S43 · Why the story survives
**Picture:** `3D: tower` Wider, the light coming up.
> So why does the tumor story survive? Partly because it's a better story. But partly, we think, because it's a comforting one. If a tumor did it, then a person like us didn't. Whitman himself seemed to want that answer. He asked for the autopsy. The evidence just doesn't give it to him.

## IV. EVERYONE'S BRAIN

### S44 · Act title
**Picture:** `GFX: title` IV · EVERYONE'S BRAIN

### S45 · The network
**Picture:** `GFX: network` Brain outline; seventeen dots appear in scattered places; lines draw from every dot to one shared network that lights up. [D1, D2]
> Here's where it gets harder. In 2018, a team led by the neurologist Ryan Darby gathered seventeen cases where a brain lesion appeared and criminal behavior followed. The lesions were in different places. But when the team mapped how those places were wired, every one connected to the same network, regions involved in moral judgment and in weighing value. The authors added a warning: most people with damage in that network never commit a crime.

### S46 · Dementia
**Picture:** `GFX: bars` Bar chart: behavioral-variant FTD 37.4% · Alzheimer's 7.7%. Source line Liljegren 2015, UCSF. [D4]
> And it isn't only tumors. Researchers at the University of California, San Francisco, reviewed more than two thousand dementia patients. Among those with one form, behavioral-variant frontotemporal dementia, more than a third developed new criminal behavior: theft, traffic violations, sexual advances, trespassing. Among Alzheimer's patients, it was under eight percent. For about one in seven people with that form of dementia, a crime was the first sign that anything was wrong.

### S47 · All the causes
**Picture:** `3D: scale` Cards pour onto the left pan; it bottoms out.
> The list of brain changes that can push behavior keeps growing. And that leads to the uncomfortable part. All behavior comes from a brain. A tumor is just a cause we can see on a scan. What about the causes we can't see? A childhood like Whitman's. The genes you were dealt. The way your brain was wired before you made a single choice.

### S48 · Sapolsky
**Picture:** `GFX: card` ARGUMENT card: "Everything we do comes down to the luck of our biology and our environment." Robert Sapolsky, *Determined* (2023). [D5]
> The neuroscientist Robert Sapolsky argues exactly this. In his 2023 book, *Determined*, he says that everything we do comes down to the luck of our biology and our environment, and since we control neither, nobody deserves blame in the way we usually mean it. If the tumor excuses the man in Virginia, his question is: why doesn't everything else excuse everyone?

### S49 · Morse
**Picture:** `GFX: card` ARGUMENT card: "Brains do not commit crimes; people commit crimes." Stephen J. Morse, 2006. [D6]
> The strongest answer comes from a law professor, Stephen Morse. "Brains do not commit crimes," he writes. "People commit crimes." In his view, a cause can never be the excuse, because all behavior is caused. If it were, we'd have to excuse everyone, and nobody really believes that. What excuses someone is losing the capacity to respond to reasons.

### S50 · Greene and Cohen
**Picture:** `GFX: card` ARGUMENT card: "…a shift away from punishment aimed at retribution." Greene & Cohen, 2004. [D7]
> And there's a third view, from the researchers Joshua Greene and Jonathan Cohen. The law, they argue, can absorb whatever neuroscience discovers. But the science will slowly change our intuitions, and push us away from punishment as payback, toward punishment as prevention.

### S51 · Our turn
**Picture:** `3D: scale` Both pans loaded; the scale hangs level and still.
> That's the debate. Now it's our turn.

## V. THE VERDICT

### S52 · Act title
**Picture:** `GFX: title` V · THE VERDICT

### S53 · The case for guilty
**Picture:** `3D: scale` Slow orbit; the right pan dips. Lower third "THE STRONGEST CASE: GUILTY."
> First, the strongest case that he's guilty. He knew it was wrong, and knowledge is what the law has always asked about. He hid what he did, which takes planning and self-control. And by his own doctors' account, the tumor likely amplified an interest that was already in him. Plenty of people carry urges they never act on. A tumor that weakens your brakes is a lot like a bottle of whiskey, and we don't excuse drunk drivers.

### S54 · The case for not guilty
**Picture:** `3D: scale` The left pan dips. Lower third "THE STRONGEST CASE: NOT GUILTY."
> Now the strongest case that he isn't. A drunk driver chooses the bottle. He didn't choose the tumor. The change came suddenly, in his forties, along with physical signs nobody could fake, like losing control of his bladder. And it switched off and on with the tumor, twice. If self-control means anything, he lost it, and he got it back from a surgeon.

### S55 · Verdict one
**Picture:** `GFX: verdict` VERDICT: "Responsible, but far less so. He needed surgery and supervision, not prison." CONFIDENCE 70%. WHAT WOULD CHANGE OUR MIND: "Evidence he had acted on a sexual interest in children before the tumor."
> Here's our verdict. He is responsible for what he did, but far less responsible than a man who did the same thing with a healthy brain. The right response was the one that eventually happened: surgery, treatment and supervision. Prison alone would have punished him and left the tumor in charge. Our confidence is seventy percent. What would change our mind: evidence that he had acted on a sexual interest in children before the tumor appeared. Then the tumor looks less like a cause, and more like an accelerant.

### S56 · On the Tower
**Picture:** `GFX: verdict` Compact: "The tumor deserves a footnote, not an acquittal." 85%.
> And the man in the tower? The tumor deserves a footnote, not an acquittal. The record shows planning, not a loss of control. Our confidence there is eighty-five percent.

### S57 · Verdict two
**Picture:** `GFX: verdict` VERDICT: "No. A cause is not an excuse. What excuses is losing the ability to respond to reasons." CONFIDENCE 60%. WHAT WOULD CHANGE OUR MIND: "Proof that ordinary offenders' self-control is impaired almost as badly as his."
> Now the bigger question. Does neuroscience eventually excuse everyone? Our verdict is no. A cause is not an excuse. The line can't be between behavior that's caused and behavior that isn't, because all of it is caused. The line is between a person who can still be moved by reasons, by consequences, by knowing that something is wrong, and a person who can't. Most people who do harm are still on the reachable side of that line. The man in Virginia, for a while, was not.

### S58 · Sixty percent
**Picture:** `GFX: verdict` Hold on the meter at 60%; the "change our mind" line brightens.
> Our confidence here is lower, sixty percent, because this is where the science is moving fastest. What would change our mind: research showing that the self-control of ordinary offenders is impaired almost as badly as his, a difference of degree, not of kind. If that's true, the line we just drew starts to fade, Greene and Cohen are right, and the purpose of punishment has to change.

### S59 · The case stays open
**Picture:** `3D: scale` Level, slow orbit, fade toward black.
> That's our verdict. It's open, which means it can change. If you think we got it wrong, make your case in the comments. We'll answer the strongest one in the next episode.

**Picture:** `GFX: words` "NEXT CASE" over black: "Tempe, Arizona · March 18, 2018."
> Next case: a car that was driving itself, a backup driver who wasn't watching the road, and a woman crossing a street in Tempe, Arizona. When the machine kills, who's guilty?
