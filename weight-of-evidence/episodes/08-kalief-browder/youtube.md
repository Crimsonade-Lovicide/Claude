## Description

> In May 2010, Kalief Browder, sixteen, was accused of robbing a man of his backpack. He said he hadn't done it. His family couldn't pay $3,000 bail, and ten weeks later, because he was on probation, a judge held him without bail. He spent about 1,110 days on Rikers Island, much of it, by his own account, in solitary confinement, waiting for a trial that never came. He refused every plea deal, including one that would have freed him that day. In 2013 the case was dismissed.
>
> We go through the reporting on his case, New York's speedy-trial rule and why it never stopped the clock, the federal investigation of Rikers, the Supreme Court's rulings on bail and detention, New York's bail reforms and what the data show, and how England does it. Then we ask: should anyone be jailed for years before a trial? We give three verdicts and tell you how sure we are.

## Pinned comment

> Our verdicts:
> 1. The clock: no one should wait years in jail for a trial. Time held before trial needs a hard limit, counted in days, that only a judge can extend, for a stated reason (85%).
> 2. Money bail: not being able to pay should never be the reason someone is held. If someone is too dangerous or too likely to run, hold them openly, after a hearing (80%).
> 3. Kalief Browder: he should never have spent three years on Rikers. Money put him there; an automatic probation hold and a clock that never ran out kept him there (85%).
