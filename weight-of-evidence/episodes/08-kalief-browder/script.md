### S20 · The Bronx
**Picture:** `3D: courtroom_push` (Case 07 clip) The empty courtroom, slow push. Lower third "The Bronx, New York · 2010–2013". Tag RECONSTRUCTION. [B1]
> His case went to the courts of the Bronx, which were overwhelmed. In 2011, nearly three quarters of the borough's felony cases were more than six months old. That year, a hundred and sixty-five felony cases went to trial. In almost four thousand, the defendant pleaded guilty.

### S21 · Six months
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "…within six months of the commencement of a criminal action…" N.Y. Criminal Procedure Law § 30.30 (felonies). [B2, B3]
> New York has a rule meant to prevent long waits. For a felony, prosecutors have six months to be ready for trial. If they aren't, and the defense asks, the charges must be dismissed. Soon after the indictment, prosecutors told the court they were ready.

### S22 · Not ready
**Picture:** `GFX: card` typed index card, Courier: "JAN 28, 2011 · People not ready. Requesting one week." then "NEXT DATE · MAR 9, 2011". Tag RECONSTRUCTION. Source "Court file, as quoted in The New Yorker, 2014". [B3]
> But once prosecutors have said they're ready, delays caused by a crowded court don't count against them. On January the twenty-eighth, 2011, his two hundred and fifty-eighth day, a prosecutor told the court: the People are not ready, we are requesting one week. The next date the court could offer was six weeks away. Only one of those weeks counted.

### S23 · The reasons

> The court file records the reasons: the prosecutor was on another trial, or on jury duty, or had a scheduling conflict, or was on vacation. Month after month, the case was put off. By the end of 2012, it had been before eight judges.

### S31 · Rikers
**Picture:** `GFX: rows` 400 ACRES · An island in the East River / 10 · Jails / ~600 · Boys in the adolescent jail when he arrived. Label INFERENCE. Source "The New Yorker, 2014". [C1, E5]
> Rikers Island is four hundred acres in the East River, with ten jails on it. In 2010, New York prosecuted sixteen-year-olds as adults, and held them there. Browder was sent to the jail for adolescent boys, some six hundred of them.

### S32 · The findings
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "…a deep-seated culture of violence is pervasive throughout the adolescent facilities." U.S. Attorney, Southern District of New York, Aug 4, 2014. [C2]
> In 2014, after investigating the years he was there, federal prosecutors found a pattern of unconstitutional treatment of the teenagers on Rikers, and a deep-seated culture of violence. Staff used force, they found, not as a last resort, but to control the boys and to punish them.

### S33 · Forty-four percent
**Picture:** `GFX: number` "43.7%" in ink; above, mono "SUBJECTED TO FORCE BY STAFF"; below, "of 705 adolescent boys in custody, Oct 30, 2012 · at least once". Source "U.S. Attorney, S.D.N.Y., 2014". [C3]
> Of the seven hundred and five boys held there on one day in October 2012, forty-four percent had been subjected to force by staff at least once. The investigators' expert said he had never seen a higher rate.

### S34 · Six by eight

On any given day in 2013, between fifteen and twenty-five percent of the teenagers were in it. On one day that July, nearly three quarters of them had been diagnosed with a mental illness.

### S43 · June 6, 2015

If you're struggling yourself, help is out there, and it works for most people: there's a number on screen and in the description.

### S44 · Freedom before conviction
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "Unless this right to bail before trial is preserved, the presumption of innocence, secured only after centuries of struggle, would lose its meaning." Chief Justice Vinson, Stack v. Boyle, U.S. Supreme Court, 1951. [F1, H1]
> The law has long said that this shouldn't happen. In 1951, the Supreme Court wrote that freedom before trial lets the accused prepare a defense, and prevents punishment before conviction. Unless that right is preserved, it said, the presumption of innocence would lose its meaning. Bail is meant to make sure you come back to court. Set higher than that requires, it's excessive.

### S45 · The exception
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "In our society liberty is the norm, and detention prior to trial or without trial is the carefully limited exception." Chief Justice Rehnquist, United States v. Salerno, 1987. Then card RECORD: "…the shortcuts we take with those whom we believe to be guilty injure only those wrongfully accused and, ultimately, ourselves." Justice Marshall, dissenting. [F2, F3]
> But in 1987, the Court also said a person can be held without bail for being dangerous, if the government proves at a hearing, by clear and convincing evidence, that no conditions of release would keep people safe. In our society, it wrote, liberty is the norm, and detention before trial is the carefully limited exception. Justice Marshall, dissenting, wrote that the shortcuts we take with those we believe guilty injure only the wrongfully accused, and, in the end, ourselves.

### S46 · New York changes
**Picture:** `GFX: rows` 2018 · No one under 18 held on Rikers / 2019 · Cash bail ended for most misdemeanors and nonviolent felonies; "ready" requires handing over the evidence / 2020–2023 · Judges regain bail for more charges, including felonies while on probation. Label RECORD. Source "NYC Mayor's Office, 2018 · Data Collaborative for Justice, 2023 · CPL §§ 30.30, 510.10". [E5, E6, E7, B7, E8]
> New York has changed a lot since. From 2018, no one under eighteen has been held on Rikers. In 2019, the state ended cash bail for most misdemeanors and nonviolent felonies, and said prosecutors can't call themselves ready for trial until they've handed over their evidence. Since then, lawmakers have three times given judges back some power to set bail, including for felonies charged while someone is on probation. A sixteen-year-old charged today would not be sent to Rikers at all.

### S47 · Danger
**Picture:** `GFX: card` ARGUMENT: "New York continues to be the only state in the country where judges cannot consider community safety in setting bail." Judge Jeffrey Zimmerman, Bronx Criminal Court, July 2023. [F5, E6, H8]
> One thing New York didn't change. Its judges may set bail only to make sure people come back to court. They can't weigh whether someone is dangerous. A Bronx judge wrote in 2023 that New York is the only state where that's so. Prosecutors have asked to be allowed to argue danger since 2019.

### S48 · What being held does
**Picture:** `GFX: number` "+13 POINTS" in ink; above, mono "CHANCE OF CONVICTION IF HELD"; below, "felony defendants · nearly a million New York City cases". Source "Leslie & Pope, Journal of Law & Economics, 2017 · Dobbie, Goldin & Yang, AER, 2018". [G1, G2, G5]
> And being held changes the case itself. In New York City, economists comparing judges who set bail more or less often found that being held raised a felony defendant's chance of conviction by thirteen percentage points. In Philadelphia and Miami, another team found much the same, mostly through guilty pleas. Browder refused to plead. Most people don't.

### S49 · What release did
**Picture:** `GFX: bars` "RE-ARRESTED WITHIN 2 YEARS · AFTER BAIL WAS ELIMINATED · 44%" / "BEFORE · 50%". Then card RECORD: release "tended to increase recidivism for people with more substantial recent criminal histories". Source "Data Collaborative for Justice, 2023 (New York City)". [G7, G8, G11]
> When New York stopped setting bail in most cases, the people released were re-arrested less, not more: in the city, forty-four percent within two years, against fifty before. But not everyone. For people with substantial recent criminal records, release led to more re-arrests, and so it did for the kinds of cases lawmakers later made eligible for bail again.

### S50 · England
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "A person to whom this section applies shall be granted bail except as provided in Schedule 1." Bail Act 1976, s. 4 (England and Wales). Then `GFX: rows` HELD ONLY IF · Substantial grounds he'd run, offend, or interfere with witnesses / THE CLOCK · 182 days in custody before a Crown Court trial, unless a court extends it. Label RECORD. Source "Bail Act 1976 · Custody Time Limits Regulations 1987, reg. 5". [F7, F8, B9]
> England starts from the other end. By law, a defendant shall be granted bail, unless there are substantial grounds to believe they'd run, commit an offense, or interfere with witnesses. Danger is a reason to hold someone, and it's said out loud. Release doesn't depend on posting a cash bond. And England's clock counts days in custody, not promises to be ready: a hundred and eighty-two before a trial in the Crown Court, unless a court extends it.

### S51 · Fourth weight
**Picture:** `3D: scale_tip_left` The first pan recovers ground. Lower third "EVIDENCE · THE LAW CALLS IT THE EXCEPTION. TWO IN THREE PEOPLE IN JAIL ARE WAITING." [F1–G11]
> So, the fourth weight. The Supreme Court calls detention before trial the carefully limited exception. Yet two in three people in America's local jails haven't been convicted. Being held makes a conviction more likely. And where New York City released more people, re-arrests mostly fell, except among people with serious recent records.

## V. THE VERDICT

### S52 · Act title
**Picture:** `GFX: title` V · THE VERDICT

### S53 · The case for holding people
**Picture:** `3D: scale_tip_left` Lower third "THE STRONGEST CASE: SOME PEOPLE SHOULD WAIT INSIDE". Then card ARGUMENT: "You really can't measure in statistics, because we are the statistics." Jennifer Harrison, Victims Rights NY, 2022. [H7–H13]
> First, the strongest case for holding people before trial. Some people charged with serious crimes will hurt someone if they're released, and the cost of a wrong release falls on a victim, not a statistic. The Supreme Court allows detention for danger, after a hearing. While someone is held, they can't commit new crimes or skip court, and the best New York study found that releasing people with serious recent records led to more re-arrests. And Browder's case isn't really about bail. Ending cash bail wouldn't have freed him. A probation hold kept him in. What failed was the delay.

### S54 · The case against
**Picture:** `3D: scale_tip_right` Lower third "THE STRONGEST CASE: INNOCENT UNTIL PROVEN GUILTY". [H1–H6, H13]
> Now the strongest case against. A person who hasn't been convicted is presumed innocent, and every day in jail is punishment without a verdict. Money bail doesn't sort the dangerous from the safe. It sorts people who can pay from people who can't. Being held makes a guilty plea more likely, whether or not you did it. Where New York City released more people, re-arrests mostly fell. And for a sixteen-year-old in New York, being held meant a jail the federal government found had a culture of violence. If someone is truly dangerous, a judge should say so, at a hearing, and hold them for that reason, not because of what's in their bank account.

### S55 · Verdict one
**Picture:** `GFX: verdict` Kicker THE CLOCK. VERDICT: "No one should wait years in jail for a trial. Time held before trial should have a hard limit, counted in days, that only a judge can extend, for a stated reason." CONFIDENCE 85%.
