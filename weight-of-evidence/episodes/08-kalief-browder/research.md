# Research: Case 08, Kalief Browder

**Question:** Should anyone be jailed before trial because they can't afford bail?
**Anchor:** The Bronx, New York, early on Saturday, May 15, 2010, ten days before his seventeenth birthday.
Kalief Browder and a friend are walking home from a party along Arthur Avenue when police stop them: a
man in a squad car says they robbed him. Nothing is found on them. The man's account changes from "just
now" to "two weeks ago." The friend is released. Browder, already on probation, has bail set at $3,000,
which his family cannot pay, and he is taken to Rikers Island. Ten weeks later he is indicted for
second-degree robbery and, because the new charge violates his probation, held without bail at all. He
refuses every plea offer, the last of them "go home today" on time served. He waits through thirty-one
court dates, eight judges and months of "the People are not ready," much of the time in solitary
confinement, until May 29, 2013, when the case collapses because the accuser has left the country. No
trial, no verdict: about 1,110 days. He speaks publicly about it in 2014. He dies by suicide on June 6,
2015, aged 22.
**Bigger question:** Every day in the United States about 450,000 people are in local jails who have not
been convicted (BJS, 2024). Some are held because a judge found them too dangerous or too likely to run;
many because they could not pay an amount a judge set. The research says detention itself makes a guilty
plea more likely. The law in New York has since changed four times (2019, 2020, 2022, 2023); New Jersey
(2017) and Illinois (2023) have all but ended cash bail; England has never used it the American way. But
Browder's own case is harder than the slogan: money opened the door, and something else kept it shut. If
money is the wrong key, what should decide who waits in jail, and for how long?

## I. Verdicts: the strongest case on each side

Suggested verdict cards (Case 02 had three): **MONEY BAIL** (should inability to pay ever be the reason
someone is held?), **DETENTION** (should anyone be held before trial, and on what showing?), and **THE
CLOCK** (should there be a hard limit on how long someone waits in jail for trial?). Possibly a fourth,
**KALIEF BROWDER** (what failed in his case: the bail, the probation hold, the courts, or the jail?).

**The strongest case for ending detention for inability to pay.** A person not convicted of anything is
presumed innocent, and the Supreme Court has said for seventy years that freedom before trial is what makes
that presumption real. Money bail doesn't sort the dangerous from the safe; it sorts the solvent from the
poor. Four studies in four cities find that being held raises the odds of conviction by roughly ten to
fifteen points, mostly through guilty pleas, and that it does little or nothing for public safety once the
case is over. Where states have cut money out (New Jersey, New York, Illinois), far more people went home and
court attendance and re-arrest barely moved. And what "held" meant in practice, for teenagers in New York, was
a jail the federal government found to have a culture of violence. If someone is truly dangerous, hold them
openly, after a hearing, for that reason. Don't hold them because of what's in their bank account.

**The strongest case for keeping detention, and money as one tool.** Some defendants will hurt someone if
released, and the people who pay for a wrong release are victims, not statisticians. The Supreme Court allows
detention for danger with a hearing; New York alone forbids judges from considering it, which leaves money as
the only lever. The best New York study found that release reduced re-arrest for low-level cases but
increased it for people with serious recent records and for the burglary and repeat-harm cases the
legislature then put back. Detention does reduce offending and missed court dates while the case is open.
Browder himself was held mostly on a probation violation, not for money: ending cash bail wouldn't have freed
him; ending delay would have.

**What would change the verdict (candidates):** a randomized or judge-assignment study showing that release
of bail-eligible violent-felony defendants raises serious violence materially; evidence that dangerousness
hearings (federal, New Jersey) detain the poor at the same rates money bail did; New York's own updated
long-term recidivism and failure-to-appear data (DCJ's longer follow-up; DCJS); evidence on whether
detention-for-danger regimes reduce wrongful guilty pleas; for THE CLOCK, data on whether England's custody
time limits shorten detention in practice.

## K. Widely repeated but wrong (do not say on air)

- **"He was held for three years because his family couldn't afford $3,000 bail."** Only the first ~10
  weeks; then remanded without bail on a probation violation (T5, A7). The single most important correction.
- **"He was arrested for stealing a backpack."** He was accused of a robbery with force and indicted for
  second-degree robbery (T3).
- **"He was 16 the whole time" / "a 16-year-old held for three years."** He was 16 when arrested, 17 ten days
  later, 20 at release.
- **"He spent 800 days / two years in solitary"** as an exact figure. It's his own estimate ("about seven
  hundred, eight hundred") and Gonnerman's "about two years"; DOC never released a count (T8).
- **"He was found innocent" / "acquitted" / "exonerated."** There was no trial; the charges were dismissed
  because the prosecution could not proceed. Say "never tried; the case was dismissed."
- **"He was convicted of the truck theft."** A youthful-offender adjudication, not a conviction on his record.
- **"His death led New York City to end solitary for teenagers."** DOC ended punitive segregation for
  adolescents in Dec 2014 and the Board voted the under-21 rule on Jan 13, 2015, both before his death. His
  story, published Sept 2014, preceded and is credited in coverage of those changes (E1–E2).
- **"Kalief's Law ended bail."** "Kalief's law" was the name of speedy-trial bills; the 2019 changes to
  § 30.30 tie readiness to discovery. Bail reform was a separate part of the same budget (B6–B7, E6).
- **"Bail reform means no one is held anymore."** Judges can still set bail or remand for qualifying
  offenses, including violent felonies and, since 2020, felonies committed while on probation (E7–E8).
- **"Bail reform caused a crime wave" / "bail reform had no downside."** Neither survives DCJ 2023 (G11).
- **"Rikers is closed" / "closing in 2027."** The legal deadline is reported as August 2027; it is widely
  expected to be missed; a federal remediation manager took over key powers in 2026 (E9).
- **"Obama ended solitary for juveniles."** In federal prisons only (E4).
- **"He was held in a juvenile facility."** He was held on Rikers with adults' jails all around, in the
  adolescent jail (RNDC) and the general punitive segregation unit; New York prosecuted 16-year-olds as adults
  until Raise the Age (E5).
