## Description

> In April 1989, a woman out running in Central Park was attacked and left close to death. Within about two days, police and prosecutors had statements from five teenagers aged 14 to 16. Four described the attack on videotape. The fifth, Yusef Salaam, 15, never went on tape: before he talked, a detective told him his fingerprints could be on her clothing. There were no such prints. No physical evidence tied any of the five to the attack, and the DNA at the scene matched none of them. They were convicted in 1990. In 2002, another man's confession and DNA cleared them, and every conviction was vacated.
>
> We go through the Manhattan district attorney's 2002 filing, the NYPD panel report that disagreed with it, the research on false confessions, the Supreme Court's 1969 ruling that still lets police lie about the evidence, how England changed its law, and the ten US states that have restricted lying to children since 2021. Then we ask: should police be allowed to lie to the people they question? We give three verdicts and tell you how sure we are.
>
> Content note: this episode discusses a sexual assault and wrongful convictions. Nothing graphic is described or shown. No images of the five men, as boys or as adults, and nothing from the videotaped statements are shown.

## Pinned comment

> Our verdicts:
> 1. On the Exonerated Five: the statements should never have convicted them. The lie was one part of what went wrong; age, hours of unrecorded questioning and a tape of only the end did more (85%).
> 2. On children: police should not be allowed to lie to a child about the evidence, or promise leniency they can't give (85%).
> 3. On adults: a confession that follows a lie about the evidence should be presumed unreliable, for adults too (65%).
