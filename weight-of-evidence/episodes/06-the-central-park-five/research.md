# Research: Case 06, The Central Park Five

**Question:** Should police be allowed to lie to the people they question?
**Anchor:** New York, the night of April 19, 1989. A woman out running in the north of Central Park is
found after 1:30 a.m., beaten and close to death, with no memory of what happened. A group of more
than thirty teenagers had been in the park that night attacking strangers. Within about two days,
police and prosecutors have statements from five boys aged 14 to 16. Four of them describe the attack
on videotape, each placing himself at the scene and putting the worst of it on the others. The fifth,
15-year-old Yusef Salaam, never goes on tape: a detective writes down what he says after telling him
that his fingerprints could turn up on her clothing. A detective later admits that was a ruse. No
physical evidence ties any of the five to her. The semen at the scene matches none of them. They are
convicted in 1990 and serve between about six and thirteen years. In 2002 a man serving life for other
rapes and a murder says he did it alone. His DNA matches. The Manhattan DA asks the court to vacate
every conviction, and on December 19, 2002 it does.
**Bigger question:** US law has let police lie to suspects about the evidence since *Frazier v. Cupp*
(1969). In England, a confession won by such a lie can be thrown out under PACE (1984). Since 2021
about ten US states have stopped it for children. Is deception a tool that gets the guilty to talk, or one that makes the innocent confess,
and does the answer change when the person in the room is fifteen?

## J. Verdicts: the strongest case on each side

Probably two verdict cards (Case 02 had three): **THE CHILD IN THE ROOM** (should police be allowed to
lie to minors?) and **EVERYONE ELSE** (to adults?). Every law we found draws the line at age, and most
target the same two lies: false claims about evidence and unauthorized promises of leniency.

**The strongest case for allowing deception.** Most people police question are guilty, and most do not
want to talk. A bluff about evidence is often what moves a guilty suspect from silence to the truth,
and police associations say it yields far more true confessions than false ones. The law already has
a safety valve: a judge must find every confession voluntary, weighing age, length and tactics, and
appellate courts review that. *Frazier* has stood for over fifty years. In the case that defines the
issue, the trial judge heard six weeks of testimony and found the detectives credible, and the NYPD's
own panel found no misconduct and still thinks the boys took part. A blanket ban trades known benefits
for vague rules and years of litigation.

**The strongest case against.** The lie that matters is the one that tells an innocent person the
evidence has already convicted him; laboratory work shows it doubles false confessions and that
observers cannot tell true from false on tape. Children are most exposed: a third of proven false
confessors in one study, and more than four in ten juvenile exonerees in another. In the Central Park
jogger case, five teenagers'
statements, contradicting each other and the scene, were enough to convict twice with no physical
evidence, while the man whose DNA was on the sock went on attacking women. English courts have been
able to exclude confessions won by such lies for forty years, and English police built a method
that does without them; ten US states now restrict them for children. If a confession needs a lie to exist, it is weakest exactly where it carries most weight.

**What would change the verdict (candidates):** a field study showing deception bans cut true
confessions or clearances with no fall in false ones (we could not open the CrimRxiv paper that
tests this); reliable evidence on English confession and clearance rates after PACE; a recorded case
where the false-evidence ploy produced a true confession that nothing else would have.
