### S11 · The park
**Picture:** `3D: park_path` A park footpath at night under one old lamp post, bare trees, wet asphalt, slow drift. Lower third "Central Park, New York · April 19, 1989". Tag RECONSTRUCTION. [A1, A2]
> On the night of April the nineteenth, 1989, a group of thirty or more teenagers came into the north end of Central Park. Over the next forty minutes or so, some of them attacked strangers. A man was beaten and robbed and left unconscious. Joggers at the reservoir were set upon. Those attacks were real, and some of the five would later admit to parts of them.

### S12 · Trisha Meili
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "Trisha Meili, 29, went out to run at about 8:55 p.m. She was found about 1:30 a.m., close to death." Source "Manhattan DA, Affirmation, 2002". No map, no photo, nothing about her injuries. [A3, E8]
> Trisha Meili was twenty-nine. She went out for a run at about nine o'clock. She was found after half past one in the morning, near a footpath in the north of the park, beaten and close to death. She survived. She has no memory of the attack. For fourteen years she kept her name private. Since 2003, she has spoken under her own name.

### S13 · The rules on age
**Picture:** `GFX: rows` UNDER 16 · A PARENT OR GUARDIAN MUST BE PRESENT / 16 · QUESTIONED AS AN ADULT. Label INFERENCE. Source "NYPD panel report, 2003 · Drizin & Leo, 2004". [A5]
> Police picked up teenagers from the group that night and the next day. Under New York law at the time, anyone under sixteen could only be questioned with a parent or guardian present. At sixteen, you were questioned as an adult.

### S14 · The five
**Picture:** `GFX: rows` Five names, each landing as it is said, age in brass mono: KEVIN RICHARDSON · 14 / RAYMOND SANTANA · 14 / ANTRON MCCRAY · 15 / YUSEF SALAAM · 15 / KOREY WISE · 16. Label RECORD. Source "Manhattan DA, Affirmation, 2002". No photos. [Names, A4]
> Five of them were questioned about the attack on Trisha Meili. Kevin Richardson and Raymond Santana were fourteen. Antron McCray and Yusef Salaam were fifteen. Korey Wise was sixteen, so he was questioned with no parent in the room.

### S15 · What the camera caught
**Picture:** `3D: interview_room` Reprise from a different angle: the camera's red tally light, the empty chair. Tag RECONSTRUCTION. Then card INFERENCE: "…the earlier interrogation sessions had not been taped." Drizin & Leo, *N.C. L. Rev.*, 2004. [A7, A8, A9]
> Prosecutors came to the precinct, and the district attorney's office recorded the final statements on video. The Manhattan district attorney, Robert Morgenthau, was a pioneer of taping confessions. But the camera caught only the end. The hours of questioning before it weren't recorded, and what happened in them is still disputed.

### S16 · The one lie on the record
**Picture:** `GFX: card` INFERENCE: "…changed his story after the detective said falsely … that his fingerprints were found on the victim's pants." New York Times, on trial testimony. Under it, small mono: "Other accounts: 'would be found' · 'if … found'". [B1, B2, T2]
> Yusef Salaam first said he hadn't been there. Then a detective told him his fingerprints could be on her clothing. Accounts differ on the exact words, but there were no such prints. Salaam then gave an account, which the detective wrote down. He never signed it, and he has always said he never confessed.

### S17 · What they said
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "None of them admitted actually raping the Central Park jogger, but each gave an account of events in which he made himself an accomplice to the crime." Manhattan DA, Affirmation, 2002. [B8, T2]
> On tape, none of the four admitted the rape. Each gave an account in which, in the district attorney's later words, he made himself an accomplice, and named the others.

### S18 · Their side

> Before trial, the boys and their parents testified that detectives yelled at them, called them liars, and that some were told they could go home if they talked. The judge didn't believe them. He found no improper trickery, and allowed every statement in, except one remark.

### S19 · First weight
**Picture:** `3D: scale_tip_left` One pan drops. Lower third "EVIDENCE · FIVE BOYS, HOURS UNRECORDED, ONE LIE ON THE RECORD". [A8, B1, B3]

### S21 · Two trials
**Picture:** `GFX: rows` AUG 1990 · MCCRAY, SALAAM, SANTANA CONVICTED / DEC 1990 · RICHARDSON CONVICTED; WISE CONVICTED, BUT NOT OF RAPE. Label RECORD. Source "Manhattan DA, Affirmation, 2002". [C1, C2]
> There were two trials in 1990. In August, Antron McCray, Yusef Salaam and Raymond Santana were convicted of rape, assault, robbery and riot. In December, Kevin Richardson was convicted on every count. Korey Wise was convicted of assault, sexual abuse and riot, but not of rape.

### S22 · Almost entirely
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "The People's case at both trials rested almost entirely on the statements made by the defendants." Manhattan DA, Affirmation, 2002. [C3]
> Thirteen years later, the district attorney's own office looked back and said it plainly: at both trials, the case rested almost entirely on the statements.

### S23 · No physical link
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "There proved to be no physical or forensic evidence … which connected the defendants to the attack on the jogger." Manhattan DA, Affirmation, 2002. [B12, B13]
> There was no physical or forensic evidence connecting them to the attack on Trisha Meili. Semen found at the scene came from one man, and before trial it was known to match none of the five, and none of the boys they had named. Prosecutors argued there must have been another attacker who got away.

### S24 · They didn't agree
**Picture:** `GFX: rows` mono, landing one by one: WHO STARTED IT · WHO KNOCKED HER DOWN · WHO HELD HER · WHAT WEAPONS · WHEN IT HAPPENED. Label RECORD. Source "Manhattan DA, Affirmation, 2002, ¶86". [B9]
> And the statements didn't agree. When the district attorney's office reviewed them in 2002, it found they differed on virtually every major part of the crime: who started it, who knocked her down, who held her, what weapons were used, and when it happened.

### S25 · Where
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "Perhaps most significant, none of the defendants accurately described where the attack on the jogger took place." Manhattan DA, Affirmation, 2002. [B7, B10, B11]
> Most significant, it said, none of them correctly described where the attack happened. Korey Wise came nearest, but police had taken him to the scene before he went on tape. Some details were simply wrong. One statement described knife wounds she didn't have.

### S26 · Why people confess
**Picture:** `GFX: number` "48%" then "94%" in brass; sub "signed a false confession · after a false witness". Label RECORD. Source "Kassin & Kiechel, 1996, as reported in Kassin, 2005". [H5]
> Why would anyone admit to something they didn't do? In a well-known experiment, students were falsely accused of crashing a computer. When someone claimed to have seen them do it, the share who signed a confession rose from forty-eight percent to ninety-four.

### S27 · No better than chance
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: observers "were not better than chance" at telling true confessions from false ones. Kassin, *American Psychologist*, 2005. [H11]
> And people watching can't reliably spot a false confession. In one study, observers who judged true and false confessions did no better than chance.

### S28 · The young
**Picture:** `GFX: number` "1 IN 3" then "42%"; subs "of 125 proven false confessions were by juveniles" / "of juvenile exonerees had falsely confessed". Sources "Drizin & Leo, 2004 · Gross et al., 2005". Then card RECORD: "Five of the false confessions in rape exonerations—more than half of the total—were in the Central Park jogger case." Gross et al., 2005. [H3, H4]
> Young people are the most exposed. In a study of a hundred and twenty-five proven false confessions, about a third came from juveniles. In another, of people wrongly convicted for crimes committed as juveniles, forty-two percent had falsely confessed. That study also counted the false confessions behind wrongful rape convictions. More than half came from this one case.

### S29 · Three in ten
**Picture:** `GFX: number` "29%" in brass; sub "of 375 DNA exonerations involved a false confession". Source "Innocence Project, 1989–2020". [H1]
> Across the three hundred and seventy-five DNA exonerations the Innocence Project has counted, about three in ten involved a false confession.

### S30 · Second weight
**Picture:** `3D: scale_tip_right` The other pan drops. Lower third "EVIDENCE · NO PHYSICAL LINK, AND STATEMENTS THAT DIDN'T FIT". [B9–B13, H1–H5]
> So, the second weight. No physical evidence. Statements that contradicted each other and the scene. And research showing that the pull toward a false confession is strongest on the young. Still, two juries heard all of it, and convicted.

### S32 · A prisoner speaks
**Picture:** `GFX: rows` LATE 2001 · A PRISONER SPEAKS UP / JAN 17, 2002 · HE SAYS HE DID IT, ALONE / FEB 2002 · THE DA'S OFFICE IS TOLD. Label RECORD. Source "Manhattan DA, Affirmation, 2002". [D1, D2]
> In late 2001, a man serving thirty-three years to life, for rapes and a murder he committed later that summer of 1989, told prison staff that someone else had been convicted of a crime he'd committed. His name was Matias Reyes. He said he had attacked Trisha Meili, and that he had done it alone. He said a chance meeting with Korey Wise, in prison, had prompted him. Wise was still inside.

### S33 · The match
**Picture:** `GFX: number` "1 IN 6,000,000,000" in brass; sub "DNA on her sock matched him · no one else's DNA was present". Label RECORD. Source "Manhattan DA, Affirmation, 2002". [D3]
> In May 2002, the FBI reported that his DNA matched the semen on her sock. Further tests matched him to the other samples, with odds of one in six billion, and to a hair. No one else's DNA was present.

### S34 · His account fit
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "…a serial rapist was also at large. The newly discovered evidence provides incontrovertible proof that he was." Manhattan DA, Affirmation, 2002. [D4, D5]
> His account fit the scene in ways none of the five statements had: the direction she was running, where she was struck, the things that were taken from her. And two days earlier, he had attacked another woman in the park.

### S35 · Every conviction
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "The People consent to the relief requested and recommend that the Court vacate the defendants' convictions in their entirety." Nancy E. Ryan, Manhattan DA's office, Dec 5, 2002. Then rows DEC 19, 2002 · JUSTICE CHARLES TEJADA VACATES EVERY CONVICTION. [D6, D7, D8, C5]
> In December 2002, the district attorney's office asked the court to throw out every conviction: not only the rape, but the robbery, assault and riot convictions too, because they all rested on the same statements, taken in the same questioning. The office still called the other crimes that night grave and inexcusable. On December the nineteenth, Justice Charles Tejada vacated them all. Korey Wise had been released that August.

### S36 · The panel
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "…it is more likely than not that the defendants participated in an attack upon the jogger." Central Park Jogger Case Panel Report, to the NYPD, Jan 27, 2003. Then card ARGUMENT: the five "should not have been totally exonerated" (paraphrase). Linda Fairstein, as reported. [E1, E2, E3, E6]
> Not everyone accepted it. In January 2003, a panel appointed by the police commissioner found no police misconduct. It concluded it was more likely than not that the five took part in the attack, and that Reyes joined in or came afterwards. It pointed out that nothing but his word said he acted alone. No court has heard that theory. Linda Fairstein, who ran the district attorney's sex crimes unit in 1989, has also disputed the exoneration.

### S37 · Trisha Meili, 2019
**Picture:** `GFX: card` INFERENCE: "I know I have no memory." / "I always knew that there was at least one more person involved because there was unidentified DNA." Trisha Meili, ABC News, May 2019. [E7]
> Trisha Meili has always said she remembers nothing of the attack. In 2019, she said she wished the case hadn't been settled, and that she had always believed more than one person was involved, because of DNA that was never identified.

> In 2014, New York City settled the men's lawsuit for forty-one million dollars, denying any wrongdoing. In 2022, an entrance to Central Park was named the Gate of the Exonerated. And Yusef Salaam now represents Harlem on the New York City Council.

## IV. THE RULES

### S40 · Act title
**Picture:** `GFX: title` IV · THE RULES

### S41 · A cousin's confession
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "The fact that the police misrepresented the statements that Rawls had made is, while relevant, insufficient in our view to make this otherwise voluntary confession inadmissible." *Frazier v. Cupp*, 394 U.S. 731 (1969). [G2]
> The American rule goes back to 1969, and a case called Frazier versus Cupp. Police had told a suspect, falsely, that his cousin had already confessed. The Supreme Court said the lie was relevant, but not enough on its own to throw out his confession. Judges should look at the totality of the circumstances.

### S42 · A mature individual
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "…the questioning was of short duration, and petitioner was a mature individual of normal intelligence." *Frazier v. Cupp*, 1969. Then card INFERENCE: "…a reasonable child subjected to police questioning will sometimes feel pressured to submit when a reasonable adult would feel free to go." *J.D.B. v. North Carolina*, 2011. [G3, G4]
> But the Court noted that the questioning had been short, and that the suspect was, in its words, a mature individual of normal intelligence. The rule was written about an adult, and for decades it was applied to children too. In 2011, the Court said age does matter: a reasonable child, it wrote, will sometimes feel pressured to submit when a reasonable adult would feel free to go.

### S43 · A fingerprint in England
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: officers "decided to invent evidence" and said "a fingerprint of his had been found in a very telling place." *R v Mason*, Court of Appeal, 1987. Then rows PACE 1984 · s. 76: A CONFESSION LIKELY TO BE UNRELIABLE MUST BE EXCLUDED, "NOTWITHSTANDING THAT IT MAY BE TRUE" / s. 78: EVIDENCE THAT HARMS A FAIR TRIAL CAN BE REFUSED. [G20, G22]
> England had already gone another way. In 1987, two years before Central Park, the Court of Appeal heard a case in which police told a suspect, and his solicitor, that his fingerprint had been found in a very telling place. They had invented it. The court ruled his confession should have been excluded. Under a 1984 law, English courts can refuse evidence that would make a trial unfair, and must exclude a confession obtained in a way likely to make it unreliable, even if it turns out to be true.

### S44 · Another way to ask
**Picture:** `GFX: rows` P · PREPARATION AND PLANNING / E · ENGAGE AND EXPLAIN / A · ACCOUNT / C · CLOSURE / E · EVALUATE. Label RECORD. Source "Kassin et al., Law and Human Behavior, 2025". [G21, G23, G24]
> England's own law came out of a false confession: three boys, aged fourteen to eighteen, confessed to a murder in 1972 they hadn't committed. After more wrongful convictions, English police built a new way of interviewing, called PEACE, around getting an account rather than a confession. According to a 2025 review by leading researchers, lying about the evidence is now barred in England, Germany, Japan, Australia, the Nordic countries and others.

### S45 · The new laws
**Picture:** `GFX: rows` JULY 2021 · ILLINOIS · OREGON / 2022 · CALIFORNIA · DELAWARE · UTAH / 2023 · COLORADO · CONNECTICUT · INDIANA · NEVADA / 2024 · MINNESOTA. Label INFERENCE. Source "Kassin et al., 2025 · state statutes". [G6–G17]
> In the United States, the change has come for children. In July 2021, Illinois and Oregon became the first states to restrict police lying to minors in interrogations. Eight more have followed. Most don't ban the lie outright. If police lie about the evidence, or promise leniency they can't give, the confession that follows is presumed involuntary, and prosecutors have to prove it wasn't.

### S46 · Adults
**Picture:** `GFX: card` INFERENCE: "In every state, police may still lie to adults about the evidence." Sources "NPR, 2024 · Kassin et al., 2025". [G5, G18]
> For adults, nothing has changed. In every state, police may still tell you they have evidence they don't. Bills to change that, in New York and Washington State, have stalled.

### S47 · Fourth weight
**Picture:** `3D: scale_tip_left` The first pan recovers ground. Lower third "EVIDENCE · THE LAW STILL ALLOWS THE LIE. THE LINE IS MOVING TO AGE." [G2–G24]
> So, the fourth weight. American law still allows the lie, and trusts judges to catch the confessions it breaks. England decided that wasn't enough, and changed how police ask questions. And ten states have now drawn a line at age.

## V. THE VERDICT

### S48 · Act title
**Picture:** `GFX: title` V · THE VERDICT

### S49 · The case for the lie
**Picture:** `3D: scale_tip_left` Lower third "THE STRONGEST CASE: LET POLICE BLUFF". [I1–I6]
> First, the strongest case for letting police lie. Most people police question are guilty, and most don't want to talk. A bluff about the evidence is often what moves a guilty person from silence to the truth. Police chiefs say it produces many more true confessions than false ones. In one lab study, pressure tactics nearly doubled true confessions. The law already has a safety valve: a judge must find every confession voluntary, and appeals courts check. In this very case, the trial judge heard weeks of testimony and believed the detectives, and the police department's own panel still believes the boys took part. A blanket ban trades a working tool for vague rules and years of litigation.

### S50 · The case against
**Picture:** `3D: scale_tip_right` Lower third "THE STRONGEST CASE: NO LIES IN THE ROOM". [I7–I12]
> Now the strongest case against. The lie that does the damage is the one that tells an innocent person the evidence has already convicted him. In the lab, a false witness nearly doubled false confessions, and a review of the research puts the odds of a false confession at roughly three to four times higher. People watching the tape can't tell the difference. Children are the most exposed. In this case, five teenagers' statements, contradicting each other and the scene, convicted them twice without physical evidence, while the man whose DNA was on her sock stayed free that summer. And English police have worked without the lie for decades.

### S51 · Verdict one
**Picture:** `GFX: verdict` Kicker THE EXONERATED FIVE. VERDICT: "The statements should never have convicted them. The lie was one part of what went wrong; age, hours of unrecorded questioning and a tape of only the end did more." CONFIDENCE 85%. WHAT WOULD CHANGE OUR MIND: "Reliable evidence, beyond the statements, that any of the five took part in the attack on Trisha Meili."
> Here's our verdict. On the Exonerated Five: the statements should never have convicted them. But the lie told to Yusef Salaam was only one part of what went wrong. Their ages, hours of questioning nobody recorded, and a tape that caught only the end did more. Our confidence is eighty-five percent. What would change our mind: reliable evidence, beyond the statements, that any of the five took part in the attack on Trisha Meili.

### S52 · Verdict two
**Picture:** `GFX: verdict` Kicker THE CHILD IN THE ROOM. VERDICT: "No. Police should not be allowed to lie to children about the evidence, or promise them leniency they can't give." CONFIDENCE 85%. WHAT WOULD CHANGE OUR MIND: "Field evidence that the new state laws cost true confessions in serious cases without reducing false ones."
> On children: no. Police should not be allowed to lie to a child about the evidence, or promise leniency they can't give. Our confidence is eighty-five percent. What would change our mind: evidence from the states that have banned it that the laws cost true confessions in serious cases, without reducing false ones.

### S53 · Verdict three
**Picture:** `GFX: verdict` Kicker EVERYONE ELSE. VERDICT: "Not about the evidence. A confession that follows a lie about the evidence should be presumed unreliable, for adults too." CONFIDENCE 65%. WHAT WOULD CHANGE OUR MIND: "Evidence from England or elsewhere that serious crimes go unsolved because police can't bluff."
> And for adults. Our verdict: police shouldn't be allowed to lie about the evidence. A confession that follows that lie should be presumed unreliable, for adults too, as it now is for children in ten states. Our confidence is lower, sixty-five percent, because adults are less vulnerable, and the evidence of harm comes mostly from the lab and from the young. What would change our mind: evidence from England or elsewhere that serious crimes go unsolved because police can't bluff.
