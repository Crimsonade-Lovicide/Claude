## I. THE BOAT

### S10 · Act title
**Picture:** `GFX: title` I · THE BOAT

### S11 · The yacht
**Picture:** `3D: sea_swell` Wide, grey ocean, long swell, no boat. Caption "SOUTHAMPTON → SYDNEY · MAY 19, 1884". [A1, A2, A3]
> The Mignonette was a fifty-two-foot yacht built for cruising near the coast, not for crossing oceans. A lawyer in Sydney had bought her in England and needed her sailed out to Australia. Tom Dudley, thirty-one, took the job, with a mate, Edwin Stephens, a sailor, Edmund Brooks, and a cabin boy. They left Southampton on May the nineteenth, 1884.

### S12 · The wave
**Picture:** `3D: sea_storm` Night, heavy swell, rain streaks. Tag RECONSTRUCTION. [A5, A8, A9]
> On July the fifth, in a storm far out in the South Atlantic, a wave smashed in the yacht's side. She sank in about five minutes. The four men got into the dinghy: thirteen feet long, with planks about a quarter of an inch thick.

### S13 · The record
**Picture:** `GFX: document` Header "THE QUEEN v. DUDLEY AND STEPHENS · SPECIAL VERDICT · EXETER, NOVEMBER 6, 1884". [SV, C8]
> Almost everything we know for certain about the next three weeks comes from one document. At their trial, the jury didn't decide whether they were guilty. It wrote down the facts and left the question of guilt to the judges. That's called a special verdict, and it's the record we'll quote.

### S14 · Rain in their capes
**Picture:** `GFX: document` Verdict excerpt, highlight on "two 1 lb. tins of turnips" and "caught in their oilskin capes". [A10]
> It says they had no water, and no food except two one-pound tins of turnips. The turtle lasted until the twelfth day. After that, nothing. When it rained, they caught the water in their oilskin capes.

### S15 · Day eighteen
**Picture:** `3D: dinghy_close` Low over the gunwale of the empty boat; a sodden oilskin, an empty tin rolling. Tag RECONSTRUCTION. [B1, A12]
> By the eighteenth day, they had gone seven days without food and five without water. Richard Parker had been drinking seawater. He was the weakest of them by far.

### S16 · No lots
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "…Brooks refused to consent, and it was not put to the boy, and in point of fact there was no drawing of lots." Special verdict. [B2]
> On the twenty-fourth, Dudley proposed that they draw lots to decide who should die so the others could live. Brooks refused. The boy was never asked. No lots were drawn.

### S17 · Families
**Picture:** `3D: sea_dinghy` Dusk section. [B3, B4]
> That evening, the record says, they spoke of their families. Dudley said that if no ship was in sight by morning, the boy should be killed. In the morning, there was no ship. Stephens agreed. Brooks did not.

### S18 · The act
**Picture:** `GFX: document` Verdict excerpt: "…the boy was lying at the bottom of the boat, quite helpless, and extremely weakened by famine and by drinking sea water, and unable to make any resistance, nor did he ever assent to his being killed." [B4, B6]
> The record says the boy was lying in the bottom of the boat, quite helpless, unable to resist. And it says he never agreed to his being killed.

### S19 · First weight
**Picture:** `3D: scale_tip_left` One pan drops. Lower third "EVIDENCE · NO LOTS, NO CONSENT, NO GREATER NECESSITY". [B10, B11]
> Four days later, the German ship Moctezuma, bound for Hamburg, picked them up. So, the first weight on the scale. The jury found that without the boy's body, the men would probably have died within those four days. It found that he would probably have died before them. And it found one more thing: there was no greater necessity for killing him than any of the other three.

## II. THE CUSTOM OF THE SEA

### S20 · Act title
**Picture:** `GFX: title` II · THE CUSTOM OF THE SEA

### S21 · Falmouth
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "Photograph of Yacht 'Mignonettes' Boat later at the Polytechnic Hall, Falmouth." Copyright form, Sept 17, 1884. [B13, B15, B16]
> On September the sixth, they landed at Falmouth in Cornwall, and they did something that startled the officials. They told them everything. Dudley even asked for his knife back, as a keepsake. Their dinghy was put on show in the town.

### S22 · The custom
**Picture:** `GFX: words` "THE CUSTOM OF THE SEA" in brass; under it "lots drawn · the loser dies · the others live". [D1]
> They talked because they believed they had done nothing wrong. Sailors had a rule for this. When a crew was starving in an open boat, they drew lots. The loser died so the others could live. It was called the custom of the sea.

### S23 · The Essex
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "…reduced to the necessity of casting lots. It fell upon Owen Coffin to die…" Owen Chase, Narrative of the Essex, 1821. [D5]
> It wasn't a legend. In 1820, a whale sank the Nantucket ship Essex. Months later, in one of its open boats, the survivors drew lots. It fell on a young sailor, Owen Coffin. He was shot, and the others ate him. The first mate published the account the following year.

### S24 · An appeal to God
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "…the selection is by lot. This mode is resorted to as the fairest mode, and, in some sort, as an appeal to God…" Baldwin J, United States v. Holmes, 1842. [D7, D8, D9]
> An American court had looked at the custom once. In 1841, after the ship William Brown hit ice, sailors threw fourteen passengers out of an overloaded lifeboat. One of them, a sailor named Holmes, was convicted of manslaughter. The judge's objection wasn't that anyone was sacrificed. It was how they were chosen. When a sacrifice is necessary, he said, the selection is by lot: the fairest way, and in some sort, an appeal to God.

### S25 · Not even that
**Picture:** `GFX: compare` Two columns. THE CUSTOM: "lots drawn · everyone at risk". THE MIGNONETTE: "no lots · the boy not asked · the weakest chosen". [B2, B4]
> In the Mignonette, there was no appeal to anyone. There were no lots. The boy wasn't asked. By the sailors' own custom, he was owed a fair chance, and he didn't get one.

### S26 · Brooks
**Picture:** `GFX: card` UNCERTAIN: "Stephens agreed to the act, but Brooks dissented from it." Special verdict. Small line: "Dudley later said all three agreed." [B9, C5]
> Edmund Brooks is the hardest part of the story to pin down. The record says he refused. He ate with the others. The Crown dropped the case against him, and he gave evidence against his shipmates. Dudley always said that all three had agreed.

### S27 · The town
**Picture:** `3D: sea_swell` Calm, overcast. Caption "FALMOUTH · SEPTEMBER 1884". [C3, C4]
> Falmouth took the sailors' side. Townspeople put up their bail. And Richard Parker's older brother, Daniel, himself a seaman, came to a hearing and shook their hands.

### S28 · A test case
**Picture:** `GFX: card` INFERENCE: "No one had been convicted in England for killing to survive at sea." Source: A.W.B. Simpson, Cannibalism and the Common Law, 1984. [D2, D3, C2]
> The government saw it differently. Survivors had told stories like this before, and nobody in England had ever been convicted for it. The Home Secretary, Sir William Harcourt, decided to prosecute. This time, a court would settle whether hunger could ever be a defence to murder.

### S29 · The name
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: Poe, The Narrative of Arthur Gordon Pym, 1838: a castaway named Richard Parker draws the short splinter. [D10, D11]
> One detail sounds invented, and isn't. Forty-six years earlier, Edgar Allan Poe wrote a novel in which shipwrecked men draw lots, and the man who loses is killed and eaten. His name is Richard Parker. If it sounds familiar, it's also the name of the tiger in Life of Pi.

### S30 · Second weight
**Picture:** `3D: scale_swing` The pans see-saw. Lower third "EVIDENCE · A CUSTOM THEY BELIEVED IN, AND DIDN'T FOLLOW".
> So, the second weight. A custom that sailors believed in, and a town that forgave them. But the custom asked for a fair lottery, and the boy never got one.

## III. THE JUDGES

### S31 · Act title
**Picture:** `GFX: title` III · THE JUDGES

### S32 · Exeter
**Picture:** `GFX: timeline` NOV 6, 1884 · EXETER · SPECIAL VERDICT / DEC 9, 1884 · LONDON · FIVE JUDGES. [C6, C7, C8, C9]
> The trial opened at Exeter on November the sixth. The defence, paid for by public subscription, argued that the weakest must go to the wall. The judge, Baron Huddleston, made sure the decision wouldn't rest with a sympathetic jury. He had them return a special verdict, a device that hadn't been used in about a century, and sent the question to five judges in London.

### S33 · Wilful murder
**Picture:** `GFX: document` Law report header "(1884) 14 Q.B.D. 273" and the line "…the prisoners' act in this case was wilful murder…". [C10]
> On December the ninth, the Lord Chief Justice, Lord Coleridge, gave their answer. It was unanimous. The killing was wilful murder.

### S34 · The highest duty
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "To preserve one's life is generally speaking a duty, but it may be the plainest and the highest duty to sacrifice it." Lord Coleridge CJ, 1884. [C11]
> His reasoning still gets quoted in courts today. To preserve one's life, he said, is generally a duty. But it may be the plainest and the highest duty to sacrifice it. Soldiers do it. Sailors are expected to do it for their passengers.

### S35 · Who is to be the judge
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "Who is to be the judge of this sort of necessity? By what measure is the comparative value of lives to be measured? Is it to be strength, or intellect, or what?" Lord Coleridge CJ. [C12]
> Then he asked the question at the centre of this case. Who is to be the judge of this sort of necessity? By what measure is the value of one life against another to be measured? Is it to be strength, or intellect, or what?

### S36 · The weakest
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "…the principle leaves to him who is to profit by it to determine the necessity… In this case the weakest, the youngest, the most unresisting, was chosen." [C13]
> His point was simple. A necessity defence lets the person who gains from the killing decide that it was necessary. And in this case, he said, the weakest, the youngest, the most unresisting, was chosen.

### S37 · Standards we cannot reach
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "We are often compelled to set up standards we cannot reach ourselves, and to lay down rules which we could not ourselves satisfy." [C15]
> And then he admitted something judges rarely say out loud. We are often compelled, he wrote, to set up standards we cannot reach ourselves, and to lay down rules which we could not ourselves satisfy.

### S38 · Six months
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: Home Office file HO 144/141/A36934: "SENTENCE: Death · Commuted to 6 months". [C17, C18]
> He sentenced them to death. The jury had recommended mercy, and within days the sentence was commuted to six months in prison. The government's own law officers thought that if it had been manslaughter, no judge would have given more than three.

### S39 · Lord, lay not this sin
**Picture:** `GFX: words` The gravestone inscription as text on black: "LORD LAY NOT THIS SIN TO THEIR CHARGE" · "Memorial to Richard Parker · Southampton". [C19, C20, D12]
> They were released in the spring of 1885. Dudley moved to Sydney, and died there in a plague outbreak in 1900. He never accepted that what he did was wrong. In Southampton, Richard Parker's memorial carries a line from the Bible. Lord, lay not this sin to their charge.

### S40 · Third weight
**Picture:** `3D: scale_tip_right` The other pan drops. Lower third "EVIDENCE · NO ONE MAY DECIDE WHOSE LIFE COUNTS LESS".
> So, the third weight. The court said nobody may decide that another person's life is worth less than their own, and call it necessity. Then the state showed mercy anyway.

## IV. WHO DECIDES

### S41 · Act title
**Picture:** `GFX: title` IV · WHO DECIDES

### S42 · Still the law
**Picture:** `GFX: timeline` 1884 · DUDLEY AND STEPHENS / 1987 · R v HOWE · NOT EVEN A THREAT OF DEATH / TODAY · NO NECESSITY DEFENCE TO MURDER. [E2, E4]
> The rule still stands in England. In 1987, the House of Lords held that even a threat to your own life is no defence to murder, and relied on this case to do it.

### S43 · Jodie and Mary
**Picture:** `GFX: words` "JODIE AND MARY · 2000" in brass; sub "Court of Appeal". [E5]
> But in 2000, the rule met a case it couldn't hold. Jodie and Mary were conjoined twins. Mary could not live on her own. Doctors said that, left joined, both would probably die within months. Separating them would save Jodie and kill Mary. The Court of Appeal said the operation was lawful.

### S44 · Self-designated
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "Mary is, sadly, self-designated for a very early death." Brooke LJ, Re A (Conjoined Twins), 2000. [E5, E6]
> One judge, Lord Justice Brooke, went straight back to the Mignonette. Coleridge had asked who decides, and by what measure. Here, Brooke said, nobody chose Mary. Her condition did. He set out three tests. The act must be needed to avoid an evil that is inevitable and irreparable. No more must be done than is necessary. And the harm done must not be out of proportion to the harm avoided.

### S45 · The rope ladder
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD (as recounted in Re A): "…petrified by cold or fear…" Zeebrugge inquest, October 1987. [E7]
> He gave another example, from the Zeebrugge ferry disaster in 1987. Passengers were climbing a rope ladder out of the water when a young man froze on it, blocking the way for at least ten minutes. An army corporal had him pushed off so the others could climb. He was never seen again. No one was prosecuted. A leading criminal lawyer thought a court could tell this apart from the Mignonette, because the young man had, in effect, chosen himself.

### S46 · The hijacked plane
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "…treated as mere objects, which denies them the value that is due to a human being for his or her own sake." Federal Constitutional Court of Germany, February 15, 2006. [E11]
> Germany faced the modern version. After September the eleventh, it passed a law letting the military shoot down a hijacked passenger plane. In 2006, its constitutional court struck it down. Killing the innocent passengers to save people on the ground, it said, would treat them as mere objects. Shooting down a plane carrying only the attackers would be another matter.

### S47 · Choice of evils
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "…could be used to justify the taking of life to avoid a far greater loss of life… the life of every individual is assumed to be of equal value." Hawaii Revised Statutes §703-302, commentary. [E9, E10]
> America's Model Penal Code went the other way. Its choice of evils rule says an act can be justified if it avoids a greater harm, and it doesn't exclude killing. Hawaii's official commentary on its version says it could justify taking a life to avoid a far greater loss of life.

### S48 · The cave
**Picture:** `GFX: words` "THE CASE OF THE SPELUNCEAN EXPLORERS · 1949" with "day 23 · the dice · rescue on day 32". [E12, E13]
> Philosophers have kept rebuilding the Mignonette ever since. In 1949, the law professor Lon Fuller invented a case: five explorers trapped in a cave kill one of their own after a throw of the dice he had proposed, then tried to back out of. His fictional judges split, and law students have been splitting over it for more than seventy years. The trolley problem, invented in 1967, is the same question with the hunger taken out.

### S49 · Fourth weight
**Picture:** `3D: scale_bottom_left` The Scale from below, one pan heavy. Lower third "EVIDENCE · THE LAW BENDS ONLY WHEN NO ONE CHOOSES".
> So, the fourth weight. The law has bent, but only in one direction. Where circumstances pick out who dies, the twins, the frozen man on the ladder, courts have found a way. Where people pick, by weakness or by usefulness, the answer is still no.

## V. THE VERDICT

### S50 · Act title
**Picture:** `GFX: title` V · THE VERDICT

### S51 · The case for the men
**Picture:** `3D: scale_tip_left` Lower third "THE STRONGEST CASE: IT WASN'T MURDER".
> First, the strongest case for the men. Four people were going to die. Three lived. The boy was dying first, and the jury said so. Nobody chose to be in that boat, and no law written on land was ever meant for it. Anyone who says they'd have done differently has never been there.

### S52 · The case against them
**Picture:** `3D: scale_tip_right` Lower third "THE STRONGEST CASE: IT WAS MURDER".
> Now the strongest case against them. They chose the weakest and youngest person in the boat, the one who couldn't object, and didn't ask him. They didn't even follow their own custom's lottery. The jury found there was no greater necessity to kill him than any of them. If that counts as necessity, then whoever is strongest decides whose life is worth less.

### S53 · Verdict one
**Picture:** `GFX: verdict` Kicker DUDLEY AND STEPHENS. VERDICT: "It was murder. The six-month sentence was mercy, and the mercy was right." CONFIDENCE 85%. WHAT WOULD CHANGE OUR MIND: "Evidence Parker was hours from death and beyond saving, so killing him cost him hours, not days."
> Here's our verdict. It was murder. They chose a boy who could not resist and did not agree, and nothing about the boat made him the one who had to die. The six-month sentence was mercy, not a verdict on what they did, and the mercy was right. Our confidence is eighty-five percent. What would change our mind: evidence that Parker was hours from death and beyond any saving, so that killing him cost him hours, not days.

### S54 · Verdict two
**Picture:** `GFX: verdict` Kicker THE COURT. VERDICT: "Coleridge was right about this boat, but 'never' is too strong a rule." CONFIDENCE 70%. WHAT WOULD CHANGE OUR MIND: "Evidence that narrow exceptions like the twins case get stretched to excuse killings like this one."
> On the court. Coleridge was right about the Mignonette. But a rule that necessity can never excuse killing is too strong, and the law already knows it. The twins case shows there are rare situations where nobody chooses the victim, and refusing to act means everyone dies. Our confidence is seventy percent. What would change our mind: evidence that narrow exceptions like that one get stretched, over time, to excuse killings like this one.

### S55 · Verdict three
**Picture:** `GFX: verdict` Kicker THE BIGGER QUESTION. VERDICT: "Killing an innocent person to save yourself is never justified. Sometimes it can be forgiven." CONFIDENCE 65%. WHAT WOULD CHANGE OUR MIND: "A case where everyone agreed to a fair lottery in advance, and all would certainly have died without it."
> And the bigger question. Is killing to survive murder? Our verdict: killing an innocent person to save yourself is never justified. Sometimes, it can be forgiven. That's the difference between saying an act was right and saying the person who did it deserves mercy, and it's exactly what England did in 1884. Our confidence is lower, sixty-five percent, because there's a hard case we can't fully answer. What would change our mind: a case where everyone agreed to a fair lottery in advance, and all of them would certainly have died without it. That's the hardest version of this question, and the Mignonette never reached it.
