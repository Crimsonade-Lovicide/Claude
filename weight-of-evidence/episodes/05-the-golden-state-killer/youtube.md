## Description

> In April 2018, detectives in California swabbed the door handle of a parked car and took a tissue from a trash can. The DNA matched a double murder from 1980. The man it belonged to, the Golden State Killer, had murdered thirteen people and attacked dozens more between the mid-1970s and 1986, and he had never been in a police database. He was found through his relatives: cousins who had uploaded their DNA to genealogy websites. None of them was asked.
>
> We go through the court record, how the search was really done, how far a family tree can reach, the false leads and the exoneration the method has produced, and the first laws written to control it. Then we ask: should police be allowed to search your family's DNA? We give three verdicts and tell you how sure we are.
>

## Pinned comment

> Our verdicts:
> 1. On the Golden State Killer: catching him this way was right; doing it in secret, on sites that hadn't warned their users, was not (80%).
> 2. On the police: yes, but only for murder and rape, with a judge's approval each time, on sites that ask their users first (75%).
> 3. No one can fully consent for a family, so the limits should be set by law, not by whoever owns the database (70%).
