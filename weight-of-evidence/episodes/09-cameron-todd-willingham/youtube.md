## Description

> In December 1991, a house fire in Corsicana, Texas, killed three young sisters. Fire investigators told a jury the burn marks proved someone had poured a liquid and lit it, and in 1992 their father, Cameron Todd Willingham, was sentenced to death. Four days before his execution in February 2004, a fire scientist sent the parole board and the governor's office a report saying most of those conclusions were invalid. The board voted 15–0 against clemency, and Texas executed him. Independent fire scientists, including one hired by a Texas commission, have since concluded the evidence never showed arson. His conviction still stands, and the children's mother says he confessed to her.
>
> We go through the court record, the fire reports and the reviews that came after, the clemency file, the Texas Forensic Science Commission's findings, and what the law says about claims of innocence after a trial. Then we ask: should a state execute someone when the evidence is disputed? We give three verdicts and tell you how sure we are.

## Pinned comment

> Our verdicts:
> 1. The fire evidence: the signs the jury heard were not evidence of arson, by today's science or by the best fire science of 1991 (90%).
> 2. February 17, 2004: Texas should not have carried out the execution that day; it should have used the 30-day reprieve to test the new science (85%).
> 3. The question: no state should execute while a credible, specific challenge to the evidence hasn't been heard by someone competent to judge it (80%).
