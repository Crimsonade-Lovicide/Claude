# Research: Case 07, Terri Schiavo

**Question:** Who should decide when to stop keeping someone alive?
**Anchor:** St. Petersburg, Florida, about 5:40 a.m. on February 25, 1990. Terri Schiavo, 26, collapses in
the hallway of the apartment she shares with her husband, Michael. Her heart has stopped. Paramedics
restart it, but her brain has gone too long without oxygen. She never recovers. She is fed through a
tube; doctors diagnose a persistent vegetative state. She left nothing in writing. For three years her
husband and her parents, Bob and Mary Schindler, care for her together. In 1993 they stop speaking. In
May 1998 Michael Schiavo asks a court to authorize removing the feeding tube, saying she would not have
wanted to live like this. Her parents oppose him. Over the next seven years a Florida trial judge,
George Greer, rules for the husband; the appeals courts affirm him again and again; the tube is removed
three times; the Florida Legislature and Governor Jeb Bush pass a law to put it back, which the Florida
Supreme Court strikes down; and in March 2005 Congress passes, and President George W. Bush signs, a law
for this one family. The federal courts decline to intervene. She dies on March 31, 2005, aged 41. The
autopsy finds a brain less than half its expected weight.
**Bigger question:** Most people never write down what they want. When they can no longer speak, someone
has to choose for them. US law asks the surrogate to make the choice the patient would have made, and lets
a state demand "clear and convincing evidence" of it (*Cruzan*, 1990). Florida puts a spouse ahead of
parents. England asks instead what is in the patient's best interests (*Bland*, 1993; Mental Capacity Act
2005). Should it be the spouse, the parents, a judge, a legislature, or the patient's own words, however
thin? And is a feeding tube medical treatment you may refuse, or basic care no one should withdraw?

## J. Verdicts: the strongest case on each side

Suggested verdict cards (Case 02 had three): **WHEN THERE'S NO DIRECTIVE** (who should decide?),
**TERRI SCHIAVO** (did the courts get her case right?), and **THE POLITICIANS** (should legislatures step
in?). Possibly fold a fourth question, "is a feeding tube treatment?", into the first.

**The strongest case for the husband and the courts.** The law already answered the episode's question
before this case began: the person decides, and when she can't speak, the people closest to her report
what she said, with the spouse first; when they disagree, a judge weighs it. That is what happened. A
trial judge heard eighteen witnesses and found her words, to three people, clear and convincing. The
appeals courts reviewed it four times. Five neurologists examined her; the court's own expert found no
awareness; the autopsy found a brain less than half its weight and blind. Every court that looked at the
legislature's and Congress's interventions found them improper or unpersuasive. To overrule all that,
you have to say either that her words count for nothing because they were spoken, not written, or that
a family's grief should outrank her.

**The strongest case for the parents.** The only evidence of what she wanted was a handful of remarks
made in her twenties, recalled a decade later by her husband and his brother and sister-in-law, after
the husband stood to inherit. The first guardian ad litem thought the husband's word alone wasn't
enough; Missouri would have required more; studies say surrogates guess wrong a third of the time even
after talking it over. Vegetative-state diagnoses are wrong surprisingly often. Her parents were willing
to care for her for the rest of her life at no cost to anyone. When the evidence is that thin and the
outcome is death, the presumption should be life, and the tube should be seen as food and water, not
machinery.

**What would change the verdict (candidates):** evidence that the credited testimony was fabricated
(none has emerged in court); a credible finding that she was minimally conscious rather than vegetative
(the autopsy points the other way but can't exclude it); research showing oral recollections of
end-of-life wishes are less reliable than surrogates' general judgments; data on how Florida's spouse-first
rule performs against England's best-interests model in contested cases.

## K. Care rules

- **She is not a symbol.** Terri Schiavo was a person whose last fifteen years became public property.
  Name her; say who she was before (a Prudential employee, married at 20, who had lost a great deal of
  weight and was trying to have a child, per Wolfson) briefly and only from the record. No speculation
  about what she felt. Do not use "vegetable" except inside the Pope's quotation, which rejects it.
- **No images of her at all.** No photographs, no stills, and above all **none of the 2002 videotapes or
  the clips cut from them** (filmed during medical exams; shown worldwide without her consent). No
  hospice, hospital or bedside imagery, no feeding tubes, no brain scans, no autopsy figures or
  diagrams of her brain. If the brain weight is shown, use a neutral number card (`RECORD`), not an
  anatomical image, and tag any generic brain model `ILLUSTRATION · NOT DATA`.
- **Both families, equal respect.** Michael Schiavo and the Schindler family (Mary Schindler; Bobby
  Schindler, who leads the Terri Schiavo Life & Hope Network; Suzanne Vitadamo) are living people with
  long public positions; Bob Schindler died in 2009. Give each side its strongest case in its own record.
  **Do not characterize anyone's motives beyond what a court or the guardian ad litem found**, and when
  quoting such a finding, quote the finding about both sides (Greer: "neither side is exempt from finger
  pointing"; *Schiavo I*: no evidence "either ... seek monetary gain"). Do not repeat the disability
