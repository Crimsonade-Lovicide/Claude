## Description

> In March 2005, the US Congress passed a law about one woman, and the President signed it within the hour. Terri Schiavo had collapsed at home in Florida fifteen years earlier, when she was 26, and never recovered. She left nothing in writing. Her husband said she wouldn't have wanted to be kept alive like that; her parents believed she was still aware. A judge decided for her husband, and her feeding tube was removed three times. She died on March 31, 2005.
>
> We go through the trial judge's ruling, the appeals, the guardian ad litem's report, the medical examiner's autopsy, the laws Florida and Congress passed for her, and how England decides the same question. Then we ask: who should decide when to stop keeping someone alive? We give three verdicts and tell you how sure we are.

## Pinned comment

> Our verdicts:
> 1. Who decides: when a family can't agree, a judge should, on the person's own words and the medical evidence. Not a legislature, and not whoever is loudest (80%).
> 2. Terri Schiavo: the courts got her case right. Her words were thin, but they were tested harder than in almost any case, and the medicine agreed (70%).
> 3. The politicians: Florida and Congress were wrong to step into one family's case after the courts had ruled (85%).
