# Case 07: Terri Schiavo

**Question:** Who should decide when to stop keeping someone alive?
**Runtime target:** 17–19 min. Narration about 2,700 words.
**Voice:** the same ElevenLabs voice and model as Cases 01–06.

Every factual line traces to `research.md` (IDs in brackets in the picture notes). Picture
notes name the tool that builds each shot:

Narration is the `>` lines only. `production/narration.py` extracts them and applies the

Care rules for this episode (research §K):
- **No images of her at all.** No photographs and none of the 2002 videotapes or clips. No hospice,
  hospital or bedside imagery, no feeding tubes, no brain scans or autopsy diagrams. The brain weight
  is a number card only.
- **Both families, equal respect.** Michael Schiavo and the Schindler family each get their strongest
  case from the record. No motives beyond what a court or the guardian ad litem found, and when we
  quote such a finding, we quote it about both sides (S15).
- **Names.** "Terri Schiavo" on first mention; her family uses "Terri Schindler Schiavo", said once
  (S11). Courts are quoted as written ("Theresa", "Mrs. Schiavo").
- **Words.** Never "coma for fifteen years", "brain dead", "pulled the plug", "starved", "vegetable"
  (outside a quotation that rejects it), or "Michael Schiavo decided" (a judge decided, S24). The
  question says "stop keeping someone alive", not "life support" (research T8).
- **Left out:** the abuse and strangulation allegations (no evidence, research A4), the family's
  deposition answers to lawyers' hypotheticals, Michael Schiavo's later family except as below, the
  disability statement's line about his motives, the 2005 Senate "talking points" memo.
- **Politicians** appear only in their own dated words. Public reaction comes from polls.
- **Reconstructions:** any courtroom or Capitol is empty and tagged.

## I. THE COLLAPSE

### S10 · Act title
**Picture:** `GFX: title` I · THE COLLAPSE

### S11 · Before
**Picture:** `GFX: rows` 1984 · Married Michael Schiavo / 1986 · Moved to Florida; her family followed / FEB 25, 1990 · Her heart stopped. Label RECORD. Source "Judge Greer's order, 2000 · Wolfson report, 2003". [A1, A2, Names]
> Terri Schiavo grew up near Philadelphia. She married Michael Schiavo at twenty and moved with him to Florida, where her parents and her brother and sister followed. She worked for an insurance company. Her family calls her Terri Schindler Schiavo. Early on February the twenty-fifth, 1990, her husband heard a thump and found her on the floor of their hallway. Her heart had stopped.

### S12 · Not a coma
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "…is not simply a coma. She is not asleep. She has cycles of apparent wakefulness and apparent sleep without any cognition or awareness." Florida Second District Court of Appeal, 2001. [T3, A5]
> She came out of the coma, but not into consciousness. Doctors diagnosed a persistent vegetative state. As a Florida appeals court later put it, that is not simply a coma: she had cycles of waking and sleeping, but, the doctors found, no awareness. She breathed on her own. She was fed through a tube.

### S13 · No cause
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "…the cause of which cannot be determined with reasonable medical certainty." Pinellas-Pasco Medical Examiner, 2005. [A3]
> What stopped her heart has never been established. Fifteen years later, the medical examiner said it couldn't be determined.

### S14 · Partners
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "…complete trust, mutual caring, explicit love and a common goal." Jay Wolfson, guardian ad litem, report to Governor Bush, 2003. [A5, A6, A7]
> For three years, her husband and her parents cared for her together. A guardian appointed by the court later described them as virtual partners, with complete trust and a common goal. Michael Schiavo, now her legal guardian, took her to California for an experimental brain implant. It didn't help.

### S15 · The split
**Picture:** `GFX: rows` 1993 · Malpractice award: most of it held in trust for her care / FEB 14, 1993 · The families stop speaking. Then card RECORD: "…neither side is exempt from finger pointing as to possible conflicts of interest." Judge George Greer, 2000. [A8, A9, B2]
> In 1993, a malpractice case brought money: around seven hundred thousand dollars, held in a court-supervised trust for her care, and three hundred thousand for her husband. That February the two families stopped speaking. Each came to accuse the other of acting for money. The trial judge's view was that neither side was exempt from finger pointing.

### S16 · 1994
**Picture:** `GFX: rows` 1994 · A do-not-resuscitate order; the nursing home objects / 1994 · Her parents try to remove him as guardian; dismissed. Label RECORD. Source "Judge Greer's order, 2000 · Wolfson report, 2003". [B1]
> In 1994, on medical advice, he declined treatment for an infection and put in a do-not-resuscitate order. When her nursing home objected, he backed down. Her parents went to court to have him removed as her guardian. They failed. Their hope, the judge later found, was that he would divorce her and let them become her guardians. They had also encouraged him to get on with his life, and in time he did: he had a new partner, and children with her.

### S17 · The petition
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "Petition for Authorization to Discontinue Artificial Life Support." Filed by Michael Schiavo, guardian, May 11, 1998. [B3, B4]
> In May 1998, eight years after her collapse, Michael Schiavo asked a court to let her feeding tube be removed. He said she wouldn't have wanted to be kept alive like that. Her parents opposed him.

### S18 · First weight
**Picture:** `3D: scale_tip_left` One pan drops. Lower third "EVIDENCE · NO WRITTEN WISHES, AND TWO FAMILIES WHO NO LONGER SPOKE". [B6, A9]
> So, the first weight on the scale. A young woman with nothing in writing. A husband who was her legal guardian. Parents who believed she was still there. And two families who no longer spoke.

## II. THE TRIAL

### S19 · Act title
**Picture:** `GFX: title` II · THE TRIAL

### S20 · Clearwater
**Picture:** `3D: courtroom` An empty courtroom, morning light through tall windows, the bench and witness stand, slow push. Lower third "Clearwater, Florida · January 2000". Tag RECONSTRUCTION. [B5, B6, B10]
> In January 2000, Judge George Greer heard the case, without a jury: eighteen witnesses, over a week. There was nothing in writing. The question the law put to him was not what was best for her, but what Terri Schiavo herself would have chosen. Florida's courts had put it this way: you don't exercise another person's right to decide by choosing what the state, the family or public opinion would prefer.

### S21 · Her words
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "…if I ever go like that just let me go. Don't leave me there. I don't want to be kept alive on a machine." Her statement as recounted by Scott Schiavo, as found by Judge Greer, 2000. [B7, T4]
> Three witnesses described things she had said: her husband, his brother and his sister-in-law. At a funeral lunch, her brother-in-law testified, she said: if I ever go like that, just let me go. Don't leave me there. I don't want to be kept alive on a machine.

### S22 · Her mother's account
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: The court found the remarks about Karen Ann Quinlan were made when Terri was 11 or 12, and gave them no weight. Judge Greer, 2000. [B8, T5]
> Her mother testified that Terri, watching the news about Karen Ann Quinlan, a young woman kept alive on a respirator, had said they should leave her alone. The judge found Terri would have been eleven or twelve at the time, and gave it no weight.

### S23 · Clear and convincing
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "…is reliable, is creditable and rises to the level of clear and convincing evidence." Judge George Greer, Feb 11, 2000. [B7, B11]
> Judge Greer found her words to the three of them reliable, and clear and convincing. In February 2000, he granted the petition.

### S24 · Not his decision
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "…the trial court essentially serves as the ward's guardian." Florida Second District Court of Appeal, 2001. [B12, G8]
> One fact matters more than any other here. Michael Schiavo did not make the decision. Because the family disagreed, he asked the court to make it in her place. From then on, the judge was in effect her guardian, deciding what she would have chosen.

### S25 · Two days
**Picture:** `GFX: rows` APR 24, 2001 · Feeding tube removed / APR 26, 2001 · Another judge orders it restored. Label RECORD. Source "Florida Second District Court of Appeal, 2001". [C1, C3]
> The appeals court upheld the ruling. In April 2001, the tube was removed. Two days later her parents went to a different judge, who ordered it put back.

### S26 · Five doctors
**Picture:** `GFX: rows` FOR HER PARENTS · 2 doctors: not vegetative / FOR HER HUSBAND · 2 doctors: vegetative / FOR THE COURT · 1 doctor: vegetative. Label RECORD. Source "Florida Second District Court of Appeal, 2003". [C5, C6]
> Her parents said she was more aware than the doctors believed. In 2002, the court heard from five doctors: two chosen by each side, and one by the court. Her parents' two said she was not in a vegetative state. The other three said she was. The judge agreed with the three.

### S27 · The videotapes
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "There are instances where she appears to respond specifically to her mother. But these are not repetitive or consistent." Jay Wolfson, guardian ad litem, 2003. No frames from the tapes. [C9]
> Short clips from those exams, of her seeming to respond and to follow a balloon, were later shown around the world. We won't show them. She couldn't consent to that. The appeals judges watched all of the tapes, and the guardian who later reviewed them found moments of response, but not repeated or consistent ones.

### S28 · Our own daughter
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "If Mrs. Schiavo were our own daughter, we could not but hold to such a faith. But in the end, this case is not about the aspirations that loving parents have for their children. It is about Theresa Schiavo's right to make her own decision…" Florida Second District Court of Appeal, June 6, 2003. [C7]
> In June 2003, the appeals court upheld the ruling again, and wrote: if Mrs. Schiavo were our own daughter, we could not but hold to such a faith. But this case is not about the aspirations that loving parents have for their children. It is about Theresa Schiavo's right to make her own decision.

### S29 · Second weight
**Picture:** `3D: scale_tip_right` The other pan drops. Lower third "EVIDENCE · A FULL TRIAL, FIVE DOCTORS, AND THE SAME ANSWER EVERY TIME". [B5–C7]
> So, the second weight. Her words reached the court years later, from her husband and his family. But a judge heard every witness, five doctors examined her, and every appeal reached the same answer. And the courts found something else: that both sides were acting out of love for her.

## III. THE POLITICIANS

### S30 · Act title
**Picture:** `GFX: title` III · THE POLITICIANS

### S31 · Terri's Law
**Picture:** `GFX: rows` OCT 15, 2003 · Feeding tube removed again / OCT 21, 2003 · Florida passes "Terri's Law"; the governor orders it reinserted. Label RECORD. Source "Bush v. Schiavo, Florida Supreme Court, 2004". [D1, D2, D3]
> In October 2003, the tube was removed a second time. Six days later, Florida's legislature passed a law written so that it fit only her case, letting the governor, Jeb Bush, order it put back. He did, that day.

### S32 · The guardian's report
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "Sometimes good law is not enough, good medicine is not enough, and all too often, good intentions do not suffice." Jay Wolfson, guardian ad litem, Dec 1, 2003. [D4, D5]
> The law also brought in a new guardian for her, Jay Wolfson. He read nearly thirty thousand pages of court records. He found the evidence of her condition compelling, and wrote that the law had done its job well.

### S33 · Our hearts are not the law
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "…But our hearts are not the law." Chief Justice Barbara Pariente, Bush v. Schiavo, Florida Supreme Court, Sept 23, 2004. [D6, D7]
> In September 2004, Florida's Supreme Court struck down Terri's Law, unanimously, for breaching the separation of powers. The chief justice wrote that the court could fully understand the family's grief. But our hearts, she wrote, are not the law.

### S34 · Palm Sunday
**Picture:** `GFX: rows` MAR 18, 2005 · Tube removed a third time / MAR 20 · The Senate passes S. 686 by voice vote / MAR 21, 12:45 A.M. · The House, 203–58; the President signs. Label RECORD. Source "House Roll Call 90, 2005 · Pub. L. 109-3". Then card RECORD: "…our society, our laws, and our courts should have a presumption in favor of life." The White House, March 21, 2005. [E3, E5, E7]
> On March the eighteenth, 2005, the tube was removed a third time. Florida's House voted to intervene again; its Senate voted no. Two days later, on Palm Sunday, the Senate passed the federal act by voice vote. The House followed just after midnight, two hundred and three to fifty-eight. The White House said that where there are serious doubts, the law should have a presumption in favor of life.

### S35 · What it did
**Picture:** `GFX: rows` IT DID · Let a federal court hear the case from the start / IT DID NOT · Order the tube put back. Label RECORD. Source "Pub. L. 109-3 · Eleventh Circuit, 2005". [E6]
> The act didn't order the tube put back. It gave a federal court the power to look at the case again, from the beginning.

### S36 · The courts decline
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "…acted in a manner demonstrably at odds with our Founding Fathers' blueprint for the governance of a free people — our Constitution." Judge Birch, Eleventh Circuit, March 30, 2005. Then card RECORD: "…death, which is imminent, effectively ends the litigation without a fair opportunity to fully consider the merits…" Judge Wilson, dissenting, March 23, 2005. [E8, E9, E10]
> The federal courts refused: a district judge, the appeals court, and then the Supreme Court, which declined to step in. One appeals judge, Stanley Birch, wrote that Congress and the President had acted in a manner demonstrably at odds with the Constitution. Another, Charles Wilson, dissented: her death would end the case without a fair chance to consider it.

### S37 · The public
**Picture:** `GFX: bars` "CONGRESS AND THE PRESIDENT SHOULD STAY OUT · 82%" / "SHOULD BE INVOLVED · 13%". Source "CBS News poll, March 21–22, 2005 (via Gallup)". [H13]
> Most Americans thought the politicians should have stayed out. In a CBS poll that week, eighty-two percent said so.

### S38 · March 31
**Picture:** `GFX: words` "MARCH 31, 2005" in ink, held on black. [E12]
> Terri Schiavo died on March the thirty-first, 2005, thirteen days after the tube was removed. She was forty-one.

### S39 · The autopsy
**Picture:** `GFX: number` "615 g" in ink; above, mono "HER BRAIN AT AUTOPSY"; below, "less than half the expected weight". Source "Pinellas-Pasco Medical Examiner, June 2005". Then card RECORD: "…cannot prove or disprove a diagnosis of persistent vegetative state or minimally conscious state." Dr. Stephen Nelson, neuropathologist, 2005. No image of any brain. [F3–F7]
> The autopsy found her brain weighed six hundred and fifteen grams, less than half what it should have. The parts that handle sight had died: she was blind. She could not have swallowed safely. She died of dehydration, the medical examiner found, not starvation. But the neuropathologist added that no examination of a brain can prove or disprove a vegetative state.

### S40 · Third weight
**Picture:** `3D: scale_tip_right` The pan sinks further. Lower third "EVIDENCE · EVERY BRANCH OF GOVERNMENT STEPPED IN. THE COURTS HELD." [D1–F7]
> So, the third weight. A governor, a legislature, Congress and the President all stepped in for one woman. Every court that looked at what they did said no. And the autopsy found what the judge had been told.

## IV. THE RULES

### S41 · Act title
**Picture:** `GFX: title` IV · THE RULES

### S42 · Nancy Cruzan
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "…a State may apply a clear and convincing evidence standard in proceedings where a guardian seeks to discontinue nutrition and hydration of a person diagnosed to be in a persistent vegetative state." Cruzan v. Director, Missouri Dept. of Health, U.S. Supreme Court, June 25, 1990. Then card RECORD: "…the citizens of Missouri to decide, through their elected representatives." Justice Scalia, concurring. [G1, G2, G3]
> Four months after Terri Schiavo collapsed, the US Supreme Court decided the case of Nancy Cruzan, a young woman in Missouri in a similar state. It said a state may demand clear and convincing evidence of what the patient wanted. Missouri did, and found that what she had said wasn't specific enough. Terri Schiavo's words were no more specific. Florida's courts found them enough. In the same case, Justice Scalia wrote that questions like these should be decided by the citizens of each state, through their elected representatives.

### S43 · Florida's order
**Picture:** `GFX: rows` numbered: 1 · A guardian appointed by a court / 2 · The spouse / 3 · Adult children / 4 · Parents / 5 · Siblings. Header "FLORIDA · WHO SPEAKS FOR YOU". Label RECORD. Source "Fla. Stat. § 765.401". [G4, G5]
> Florida's law sets an order for who speaks for you if you haven't chosen anyone: a court-appointed guardian, then your spouse, then your adult children, then your parents. And the decision has to be the one you would have made, proven by clear and convincing evidence, or, if no one can know that, what's in your best interests.

### S44 · Sixty-eight percent
**Picture:** `GFX: number` "68%" in brass; above, mono "SURROGATES' ACCURACY"; below, "predicting patients' treatment choices · 16 studies". Source "Shalowitz, Garrett-Mayer & Wendler, Arch Intern Med, 2006". [H11]
> But the people closest to us are not as good at this as we'd hope. A review of sixteen studies found that surrogates predicted patients' choices correctly sixty-eight percent of the time, and that talking it over beforehand didn't make them any more accurate.

### S45 · Best interests
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: the decision-maker must consider "the person's past and present wishes and feelings" and "the beliefs and values that would be likely to influence his decision." Mental Capacity Act 2005, s. 4 (England and Wales). [G9, G10, G12]
> England asks a different question. Since 1993, when the House of Lords let doctors stop tube-feeding Tony Bland, left in a vegetative state by the Hillsborough disaster, English law has treated tube feeding as medical treatment, and asked what is in the patient's best interests. The family is consulted, but doesn't decide. The patient's wishes and beliefs count. And if there's real disagreement, a court decides.

### S46 · Care or treatment
**Picture:** `GFX: card` RECORD: "…a natural means of preserving life, not a medical act." Pope John Paul II, March 20, 2004. Then card ARGUMENT: "…a feeding tube is a low-tech support, and people who use them can and do live full and meaningful lives." Statement of 23 national disability organizations, Oct 27, 2003. [H6, H9]
> Not everyone accepts that tube feeding is treatment. In 2004, Pope John Paul the Second called it a natural means of preserving life, not a medical act. Twenty-three disability rights groups said a feeding tube is low-tech support, and that people who use one can live full and meaningful lives.

### S47 · Misdiagnosis
**Picture:** `GFX: number` "43%" in ink; above, mono "MISDIAGNOSED"; below, "of 40 patients referred as vegetative · one London unit". Source "Andrews et al., BMJ, 1996". [H1, H4]
> And the diagnosis itself can be wrong. In one London unit, forty-three percent of patients referred as vegetative turned out to have been misdiagnosed. In her case, though, three of five doctors, the trial judge and the autopsy all pointed the same way.

### S48 · Fourth weight
**Picture:** `3D: scale_tip_left` The first pan recovers ground. Lower third "EVIDENCE · THE LAW ASKS WHAT YOU WANTED. MOST OF US NEVER SAY." [G1–H12]
> So, the fourth weight. American law tries to give you the choice you would have made. But most people never put it in writing, and the people who love them guess wrong a third of the time.

## V. THE VERDICT

### S49 · Act title
**Picture:** `GFX: title` V · THE VERDICT

### S50 · The case for her husband and the courts
**Picture:** `3D: scale_tip_left` Lower third "THE STRONGEST CASE: THE COURTS GOT IT RIGHT". [I1–I6]
> First, the strongest case for her husband and the courts. The law had already answered this question: the decision is hers, and when she can't speak, the people closest to her report what she said, and if they disagree, a judge weighs it. That's what happened. A judge heard eighteen witnesses and found her words clear and convincing. The appeals courts reviewed it again and again. Five doctors examined her, and the autopsy found a brain less than half its weight. To overrule all that, you have to say her words don't count because they were spoken, not written, or that her family's grief should outweigh her.

### S51 · The case for her parents
**Picture:** `3D: scale_tip_right` Lower third "THE STRONGEST CASE: WHEN IN DOUBT, CHOOSE LIFE". [I7–I12]
> Now the strongest case for her parents. The only evidence of what she wanted was a few remarks made in her twenties, recalled years later by her husband and his family. A guardian appointed by the court in 1998 thought her husband's word alone wasn't enough, and Missouri would have demanded more. Surrogates guess wrong a third of the time, and the diagnosis is sometimes wrong. Her parents were willing to care for her for the rest of her life. When the evidence is that thin, and the outcome is death, the law should lean toward life, and see food and water as care, not treatment.

### S52 · Verdict one
**Picture:** `GFX: verdict` Kicker WHO DECIDES. VERDICT: "When a family can't agree, a judge should decide, on the person's own words and the medical evidence. Not a legislature, and not whoever is loudest." CONFIDENCE 80%. WHAT WOULD CHANGE OUR MIND: "Evidence that judges in contested cases decide less faithfully to patients' wishes than families or doctors would."
> Here's our verdict. On who decides: when a family can't agree, a judge should, on the person's own words and the medical evidence. Not a legislature, and not whoever is loudest. Our confidence is eighty percent. What would change our mind: evidence that judges in contested cases are less faithful to patients' wishes than families or doctors would be.

### S53 · Verdict two
**Picture:** `GFX: verdict` Kicker TERRI SCHIAVO. VERDICT: "The courts got her case right. Her words were thin, but they were tested harder than in almost any case before or since, and the medicine agreed." CONFIDENCE 70%. WHAT WOULD CHANGE OUR MIND: "Credible evidence that the testimony about her words was invented, or that she was minimally conscious."
> On Terri Schiavo: the courts got her case right. Her words were thin, but they were tested harder than in almost any case before or since, and the medicine agreed. Our confidence is seventy percent, lower, because remembered remarks are weak evidence, and a life was at stake. What would change our mind: credible evidence that the testimony about her words was invented, or that she was minimally conscious after all.

### S54 · Verdict three
**Picture:** `GFX: verdict` Kicker THE POLITICIANS. VERDICT: "Florida and Congress were wrong to step into one family's case after the courts had ruled." CONFIDENCE 85%. WHAT WOULD CHANGE OUR MIND: "Evidence that the courts' process was actually deficient: that something that mattered was never heard."
> And on the politicians: Florida and Congress were wrong to step into one family's case after the courts had ruled. Our confidence is eighty-five percent. What would change our mind: evidence that the courts' process really was deficient, that something that mattered was never heard. And one thing nearly everyone in this case would agree on: write down what you would want, and tell the person you trust.

### S55 · The case stays open
**Picture:** `3D: scale_level_loaded` Level, slow orbit, fade toward black.
> That's our verdict. It's open, which means it can change. If you think we got it wrong, make your case in the comments. We'll answer the strongest one in the next episode.
