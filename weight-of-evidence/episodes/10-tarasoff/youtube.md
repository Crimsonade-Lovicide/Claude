## Description

> In the summer of 1969, according to the lawsuit that followed, a graduate student at Berkeley told his therapist he was going to kill a young woman when she came home from Brazil. The psychologist believed him, and asked the campus police to hold him for a psychiatric evaluation. They held him briefly and let him go. No one warned her family. That October, he killed Tatiana Tarasoff. Her parents sued, and in 1976 the California Supreme Court ruled that a therapist who determines a patient is a serious danger to someone must take reasonable care to protect that person: "The protective privilege ends where the public peril begins."
>
> We go through both of the court's rulings and the dissents, the criminal appeal, the laws written since, the research on predicting violence and on what confidentiality does in therapy, the one study of whether mandatory warnings save lives, and how England answers the same question. Then we ask: should a therapist break a patient's confidence to warn someone in danger? We give three verdicts and tell you how sure we are.
>
> Content note: this episode discusses the murder of a young woman, stalking, and mental illness and violence. Nothing graphic is described or shown. No images of Tatiana Tarasoff or of the man who killed her are shown. The facts about the clinicians and the police are what her parents alleged; no court ever tried them.

## Pinned comment

> Our verdicts:
> 1. Berkeley, 1969: on the facts as her parents alleged them, once the clinicians judged him dangerous enough to hold and the hold failed, someone should have made sure Tatiana or her family knew (80%).
> 2. The rule: a legal duty to protect, not a duty to warn. When a patient makes a serious threat against an identifiable person, the therapist must act, and a warning is one way (70%).
> 3. The question: yes, but only for a serious threat against someone identifiable, only when nothing less will protect them, and only as much as the danger requires (75%).
>
> The one study of mandatory warnings points the other way on verdict 2. Which verdict would you move, and what evidence would move it?
