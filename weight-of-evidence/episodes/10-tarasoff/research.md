# Research: Case 10, Tarasoff v. Regents of the University of California

**Question:** Should a therapist break a patient's confidence to warn someone in danger?
(Owner's wording, kept. §J explains why the verdict needs to separate *may* from *must*, and *warn* from
*protect*: the 1976 court did not require a warning; it required "reasonable care to protect," of which a
warning is one form.)
**Anchor:** Berkeley, California, summer and autumn of 1969. Prosenjit Poddar, a graduate student from
Bengal, India, is a voluntary outpatient at the university's Cowell Memorial Hospital. According to the
complaint the Supreme Court took as true, on August 20, 1969 he tells his psychologist, Dr. Lawrence Moore,
that he is going to kill a young woman he does not name but who is "readily identifiable" as Tatiana
Tarasoff, then spending the summer in Brazil. Moore and two psychiatrists decide he should be committed for
observation; Moore asks the campus police for help; three officers pick Poddar up, find him rational, and
release him on his promise to stay away from her. The department's director, Dr. Harvey Powelson, then asks
for Moore's letter back, orders it and Moore's notes destroyed, and orders no action to hold Poddar. On
October 27, 1969, after Tatiana has come home, Poddar kills her. No one has warned her family. Her parents
sue. The trial court throws the case out; the California Supreme Court reinstates it twice, in December 1974
and, after a rare rehearing, in July 1976, holding that a therapist who determines, or should determine, that
a patient presents a serious danger of violence to another "incurs an obligation to use reasonable care to
protect the intended victim." It is the most cited mental-health case in American law.
**Bigger question:** The case was never tried. Every fact about what the therapists did comes from a
complaint that the court had to assume was true, and the civil case settled (reportedly) before any trial.
So the episode is not about whether Moore or Powelson was negligent; it is about the rule the case left:
when a patient's words point at a real person, does the therapist's promise of secrecy end? Forty-plus states
now say yes in some form; they disagree about whether the therapist *may* or *must* act; one economist found
that the mandatory states saw *more* homicides, not fewer; and clinicians' predictions of violence remain
only modestly better than chance. England answers the question differently: a doctor *may* disclose to
protect the public (W v Egdell, 1989), but there is no Tarasoff-style duty.

## E. The science and practice: predicting violence, and what confidentiality does

| ID | Claim | Tag | Sources |

| E1 | **1976: the profession said it couldn't predict.** The APA's amicus "cites numerous articles which indicate that therapists, in the present state of the art, are unable reliably to predict violent acts; their forecasts, amicus claims, tend consistently to overpredict violence, and indeed are more often wrong than right" (the court's summary, verbatim). ## J. Verdicts: candidates

Suggested verdict cards (Case 02 had three). **We rule on the rule, not on whether Moore or Powelson was
negligent: the facts were never tried.**

1. **BERKELEY, 1969** (on the facts as alleged, should someone have told Tatiana or her family?). Candidate:
   *Yes. Once the clinicians judged him dangerous enough to hold, and the hold failed, someone should have made
   sure she knew.* Confidence ~80%. What would change our mind: evidence that Tatiana or her family already
   knew of the threat (in three-quarters of contacted victims in [BM96], they did); evidence that a warning
   would have provoked rather than prevented the attack; the trial record showing the August chronology is
   wrong and the threat came later.
2. **THE RULE** (should therapists have a legal duty to protect an identifiable person from a serious threat?).
   Candidate: *Yes, a duty to protect, not a duty to warn: when a patient makes a serious threat against an
   identifiable person, the therapist must act, and warning is one way.* Confidence ~70%. What would change our
   mind: a replicated study showing that duty states (mandatory or common-law) have more violence or less
   treatment-seeking than permissive states; evidence that the duty mainly produces commitments rather than
   protection.
3. **THE QUESTION** (should a therapist break a patient's confidence to warn someone in danger?). Candidate:
   *Yes, but only for a serious threat to an identifiable person, only when nothing less will work, and only
   as much as the danger requires. That is a narrow exception, not a reporting rule.* Confidence ~75%. What
   would change our mind: good evidence that patients who know the limit stop disclosing threats (the evidence
   we have is analogue and decades old, E6); or that permissive regimes routinely leave identifiable victims
   unwarned.
4. Optional: **MUST, OR MAY?** (should the law *require* a warning?). Candidate: *No: the law should allow
   it and protect the therapist who does it, but the one study of outcomes gives no support to requiring it.*
   Confidence ~55% (low, because the evidence is one study). What would change our mind: replication of
   Edwards that finds no effect, or a study showing mandatory states warn more victims who are then saved.

## L. Widely repeated but wrong (do not say on air)

- **"Tarasoff created a duty to warn."** The 1974 opinion did; it was vacated. The 1976 opinion, the law,
  created a duty "to use reasonable care to protect," of which warning is one option (D4). (California's
  statute said "warn and protect" until Jan 1, 2013, G3.)
- **"The court found the therapists (or the university) liable."** It held only that the parents could state
  a claim; no one was ever found liable; the case reportedly settled (D11).
- **"The university paid $X."** No amount is in any source we found (D11).
- **"The police were liable" / "had a duty to warn."** The 1976 court affirmed judgment for the police (D8).
- **"Poddar told his therapist her name."** The complaint says "an unnamed girl, readily identifiable as
  Tatiana" (T3).
- **"The therapist did nothing" / "kept it secret."** He sought commitment and told the police (T4).
- **"Moore and Powelson wrote to the police together"** (Psychology Today). Moore wrote; Powelson asked for the
  letter back (B3).
- **"No one warned her" as a court finding.** The governing opinion says no one warned her parents; whether
  she was warned was never alleged (T6).
- **"Two months after he told his therapist" as settled fact.** The two opinions disagree (T7).
- **"Poddar was acquitted / found insane / convicted of manslaughter."** Convicted of second-degree murder and
  found sane; the Court of Appeal reduced it to manslaughter; the Supreme Court reversed for retrial; no
  retrial (C2–C6).
- **"He served five years."** About four years three months had passed when the conviction was reversed; the
  release date is unknown (C7).
- **"He was deported" as an official order.** Secondary sources say released on condition he return to
  India; the legal mechanism is unconfirmed (C6).
- **"Every state has a duty to warn" / "33 states" / "only four states have no duty"** as a current fact.
  Counts differ by method and date; the current NCSL count is unverified (G6).
- **"HIPAA requires therapists to warn."** It permits disclosure (G8).
- **"Studies prove duty-to-warn laws increase homicides."** One study, observational, unreplicated, with the
  caveats in H2.
- **"Therapists can't predict violence at all."** Better than chance, modestly; tools are good at
  identifying low risk and poor at confirming high risk (E2–E3).
- **"People with mental illness are violent."** About one in twenty violent crimes; far more often victims
  (F1–F3).
- **"Tarasoff applies in England."** England permits disclosure in the public interest (Egdell); it imposes
  no Tarasoff duty (H3, H5).
- **"The protective privilege ends where the public peril begins" was overturned with the 1974 opinion.** The
  line is in the 1976 opinion, p. 442 (D5).
- **"Tatiana was a Berkeley student when they met."** Unverified either way (A2). Say "a student."
